Shrinking Mind

Veganism, Nutrient Deficiency, and the Cognitive Cost of a Plant-Only Diet

An essay in   nineteen chapters

Author's note

This book is a work of investigative non-fiction and critical synthesis. It draws on peer-reviewed research, clinical studies, and the contemporary nutritional literature to examine a question that the popular discourse around veganism too often refuses to ask: what does a strict plant-only diet do to the human brain over time?

Nothing in these pages is medical advice. The author is not a physician. Readers considering changes to their diet, particularly during pregnancy, lactation, infancy, childhood, or in the presence of an existing medical condition, should consult a qualified clinician.

The book is critical in tone and purpose. It does not pretend to be neutral. It engages the strongest counterarguments in good faith, but its thesis is that the brain is an animal organ, evolved on animal-derived nutrients, and that severing it from those nutrients carries a measurable cognitive cost — one that supplementation can blunt but does not always erase. Readers looking for an even-handed comparison of dietary patterns will find more balanced books elsewhere. Readers who suspect the celebratory consensus around plant-only eating has overlooked something important will find the case laid out here.

Opening

Preface

In 2008, a research group at the University of Oxford published a paper that ought to have rattled the nutritional establishment. They had followed a cohort of older adults for five years, measuring blood markers, cognitive performance, and brain volume by MRI. The finding was stark. People with the lowest levels of vitamin B12 in their blood lost brain tissue at six times the rate of people with the highest levels. The volume loss was not subtle, not within the noise of normal aging. It was a measurable, accelerated shrinkage of the organ that does the thinking.

The paper landed quietly. It did not produce a wave of public health advisories. It did not become a fixture of mainstream nutrition coverage. It did not lead, as one might naively expect, to a serious public conversation about the populations most likely to be deficient in B12. It was filed away and largely forgotten, except by the small circle of researchers who continued the work.

There is a reason for that quiet. Vitamin B12 is the single nutrient most reliably absent from a strict plant-only diet. Cobalamin, as it is properly called, is synthesized by bacteria; it accumulates in animal tissues; it does not appear in any meaningful amount in any plant food. The implications of the Oxford finding, followed in a straight line, lead to a place that the contemporary food conversation does not want to go. They lead to the conclusion that the dietary pattern most aggressively promoted as the future of human eating — plant-only, low-meat, no-animal-products — is also the dietary pattern most reliably associated with the precise nutrient deficiency that shrinks brains.

This book is the long-form examination of that uncomfortable line.

I want to be clear at the outset about what the book is and is not. It is not a polemic against plant foods. The human diet has always included more plant matter than animal matter; vegetables, fruits, grains, legumes, nuts, and seeds are not the problem and have never been the problem. It is also not a defense of the modern industrial omnivore diet, with its ultra-processed convenience foods and its disconnection from the animals it consumes. The argument here is narrower and sharper. It is that the strictest version of plant-based eating — veganism, in which no food of animal origin is consumed at all — fails the human brain in ways that the popular discussion has either ignored, minimized, or actively buried.

The case rests on a chain of evidence. The brain is a structurally and functionally demanding organ. It evolved over roughly two million years on a diet that included substantial animal matter. Several of the nutrients required to build, maintain, and operate it are either absent from plants entirely or present in forms the human body absorbs poorly. When those nutrients fall below threshold, the brain pays. The payment shows up in blood markers like elevated homocysteine; in cognitive markers like declining processing speed; in mood disorders; in developmental delays in children; and, most starkly, in the loss of brain mass itself.

I have tried throughout the book to engage the counterarguments seriously. The defenders of plant-only diets are not foolish, and their case is not nothing. Plant foods are rich in antioxidants and polyphenols; they are anti-inflammatory; they support a favorable gut microbial profile; and observational studies do show some cardiovascular benefits in plant-leaning populations. None of that is in dispute. The dispute is whether those benefits are sufficient to compensate for the specific neurological costs of nutrient deficiency, and whether supplementation is the clean solution that its proponents claim.

My answer, after working through the literature, is that the compensation is partial, that supplementation is necessary but not sufficient, and that the cognitive risks of strict veganism — particularly across decades, particularly in the most vulnerable life stages — are larger than the public conversation has been willing to admit.

The reader will judge the case for themselves. I ask only that they read the whole argument before deciding.

A note on style. The book is written for the general reader who is willing to engage with science but does not want to be drowned in citations. The most important studies are named in the text; the broader literature is gestured to in a closing notes section. Where I have simplified, I have tried not to falsify. Where the evidence is genuinely uncertain, I have said so. The tone is critical, but the goal is honest critique, not rhetoric.

The brain is not an abstraction. It is the organ that holds whoever you are. What we feed it matters more than almost anything else we do. The argument of this book is that the strictest forms of plant-only eating do not feed it well enough, and that the cost is being paid quietly, in the shrinkage of the very tissue that makes us who we are.

I

The Brain Is an Animal Organ

On evolution, anatomy, and the nutrients that built us.

Chapter 1

Two Million Years of Meat

The human brain is an expensive organ. It accounts for roughly two percent of body weight in an adult and consumes around twenty percent of the body's resting energy. Per gram, it is the most metabolically demanding tissue we own. To grow it, to maintain it, and to run it, the body needs a continuous supply of specific raw materials. Those materials did not appear by accident in the diet of our ancestors. They were the things our ancestors ate. The brain, in other words, is shaped to the food that built it.

The food that built it included meat.

This is not a controversial claim within the field of human evolution, although it has become a difficult one to say in public. The fossil and archaeological record shows a steady increase in the consumption of animal tissue across the genus Homo over the past two and a half million years. The transition is visible in the cut marks on bones, in the tools designed to butcher carcasses, in the isotope ratios of fossilized human remains, and in the parallel reduction of the gut. As our ancestors ate more nutrient-dense animal tissue, the gut shrank because it no longer needed to ferment large volumes of low-density plant matter. The brain, freed from competing for the same metabolic budget, grew. The body became taller and leaner. The hand became more precise. The face became smaller, the jaw lighter, the teeth less robust. The whole human organism was reshaped, over millions of years, around a diet that included substantial animal foods.

The point of opening the book this way is not to romanticize hunting or to construct an evolutionary morality tale. It is to make a simple observation that has gone strangely missing from the popular discussion of plant-only eating. The human brain is the product of an evolutionary process in which animal nutrients were continuously available. The biochemistry of that brain reflects that history. When we ask what happens to the brain when those nutrients are removed, we are not asking a hypothetical question. We are asking what happens when an organ is severed from the inputs it was selected to use.

Consider what those inputs are. The brain is built largely of fat, and a particular kind of fat dominates its membranes: long-chain omega-3 fatty acids, especially docosahexaenoic acid, or DHA. DHA is concentrated in fish, in the brains of land animals, and in shellfish. Plants do contain a precursor, alpha-linolenic acid, but the human body converts it to DHA only with great inefficiency. The conversion rate, in most studies, sits in the low single digits — perhaps three to five percent in healthy young adults, considerably lower in many groups. A diet rich in plant omega-3 precursors and entirely free of animal omega-3 sources is not, in biochemical terms, a diet rich in DHA. It is a diet that delivers a bottleneck.

The brain runs on glucose, but it builds itself from fat, and a striking proportion of that fat is the kind that comes from animals.

Consider the nutrient that gives this book its central thread: vitamin B12. B12 is required for the maintenance of myelin, the fatty insulating sheath that wraps around nerve fibers and allows electrical signals to travel along them at speed. It is required for the synthesis of S-adenosylmethionine, the universal methyl donor, which powers a vast number of regulatory reactions in the brain. It is required for the proper metabolism of homocysteine, an amino acid that becomes neurotoxic when it accumulates. The body needs only tiny amounts of B12, in the range of micrograms per day, but it needs them reliably. The vitamin is synthesized by bacteria. It accumulates in the tissues of animals that have eaten or harbored those bacteria. It does not appear in any plant food in a form the human body can use. There are unfortified seaweeds and fermented plant products that contain B12 analogs, but the analogs are not biologically active and may even compete with real B12 at the receptor.

This is not a defect of plant foods. It is simply a fact about where B12 lives in the food chain. For most of human history, B12 was acquired by eating animals or animal products. There was no other route.

Iron is similar. The brain needs iron for the synthesis of neurotransmitters, for myelination, and for the basic energy metabolism of the cells. Plant foods do contain iron, but in a form called non-heme iron, which the human gut absorbs at a fraction of the rate at which it absorbs the heme iron found in animal tissues. The bioavailability gap is substantial. A given milligram of iron from spinach is not interchangeable with a milligram of iron from beef. The body knows the difference, even if the supplement label does not.

Zinc, selenium, iodine, choline, taurine, creatine, carnosine — the list of nutrients that the brain depends on, and that animal foods deliver more reliably than plant foods, is longer than most people realize. We will return to each in turn. The point at this stage is the cumulative one. When you remove animal foods from a human diet, you do not remove a single nutrient. You remove an interlocking set of nutrients that the brain has been built, over evolutionary time, to require.

Defenders of veganism often respond at this point with two counterarguments. The first is that we are no longer Pleistocene hunters and we no longer need to eat as our ancestors did. The second is that any nutrient that animal foods supply can be obtained from plants, supplements, or fortified products.

The first argument confuses a moral claim with a biological one. It is true that we are no longer Pleistocene hunters in any cultural sense. We do not need to obtain calories by chasing down ungulates on the savanna. But our biochemistry has not been updated. The enzymes that struggle to convert plant omega-3s into DHA are the same enzymes our hunter-gatherer ancestors carried. The receptors that bind B12 in the small intestine are the same receptors. The blood-brain barrier and the neurons it protects are the same neurons. Cultural progress does not redesign the human body. We have built ourselves a world in which animal foods are no longer required for survival in many environments. That is not the same as having built ourselves a body that no longer benefits from them.

The second argument — that supplements close the gap — is more substantive, and it deserves a careful answer. We will return to it at length in Part Four of this book. The short answer is that supplementation works imperfectly. Synthetic cyanocobalamin is not always absorbed at the same rate as food-bound B12. Plant-based DHA from algae oil is real and useful but is consumed at much lower rates than the equivalent intake from fatty fish. Iron supplements cause gastrointestinal distress in many users and are often discontinued. Compliance with supplementation, across years and across populations, is poor. The strict vegan who reliably takes the right supplements at the right doses with the right cofactors for the rest of their life is not a hypothetical, but they are not the median vegan either. Most people who make the dietary commitment do not maintain that level of nutritional vigilance.

The argument of this book is not that veganism is impossible to do safely. The argument is that doing it safely requires a level of nutritional knowledge and behavioral consistency that most people, in practice, do not maintain — and that the brain is the organ that pays first when they do not.

We turn next to the brain itself. To understand what the missing nutrients do, we have to understand what the brain is doing with them.

Chapter 2

Anatomy of a Hungry Organ

A reader who has not spent time with neuroscience may have an intuitive picture of the brain as something like a computer: a calculating device, dense with circuitry, somehow running on electricity. The picture is not entirely wrong, but it is misleading in a way that matters for the argument of this book. The brain is not a piece of dry electronics. It is a wet, fatty, metabolically frantic mass of living cells, each of which is constantly building, breaking down, and rebuilding itself. To understand what nutrient deficiency does to the brain, we have to take seriously how much construction work the brain is doing every minute of its life.

Start with the basic numbers. The adult human brain weighs about 1.4 kilograms, roughly two percent of body mass. It consumes around twenty percent of the body's resting energy. It does this twenty-four hours a day, with no significant rest. Even in deep sleep, the brain's energy use barely drops. The metabolic rate per gram of brain tissue is roughly ten times the rate per gram of muscle. For a tissue that does not move, does not pump blood, and does not digest food, this is an extraordinary energy demand.

That demand exists because the brain is doing structural work continuously. Synapses — the junctions between neurons — are constantly being formed, strengthened, weakened, and pruned. Neurotransmitters are being synthesized, released, captured, and broken down on millisecond timescales. Membranes are being remodeled. Myelin sheaths are being maintained and, when damaged, repaired. The fluid balance is being defended against the constant electrical work of the cells. None of this is free. All of it requires raw materials.

The largest single category of those raw materials is fat. The brain is roughly sixty percent fat by dry weight, the most lipid-dense organ in the body. The membranes of neurons are made of phospholipids, and a striking proportion of those phospholipids contain long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids — particularly docosahexaenoic acid, DHA, which is so concentrated in neural tissue that some researchers describe it as a structural component of the brain rather than a nutrient. The membranes are not inert envelopes around the cell. They are the substrate on which receptors sit, on which ion channels open and close, on which the entire signaling apparatus of the neuron is built. The composition of those membranes determines, in part, how well the neuron functions. A neuron with insufficient DHA in its membranes is a neuron with a degraded signaling environment.

The next category is protein, but the brain's protein demand is more about quality than quantity. The neurotransmitters that carry signals between neurons are built from amino acid precursors. Tyrosine becomes dopamine and noradrenaline. Tryptophan becomes serotonin. Glutamate is itself an amino acid and is the principal excitatory neurotransmitter. The synthesis of these molecules is regulated by enzymes that themselves require cofactors — vitamins and minerals that the brain cannot manufacture. B6, B12, folate, iron, zinc, copper, magnesium. Each of these is required at specific points in specific synthesis pathways. A deficit in any one of them creates a bottleneck somewhere in the chemistry of mood, attention, or memory.

The third category, and the one that this book will return to repeatedly, is the methyl donor system. Many of the most important regulatory reactions in the brain involve the transfer of a single methyl group — a carbon and three hydrogens — from one molecule to another. Methylation activates and deactivates genes. It builds neurotransmitters. It maintains the integrity of myelin. The supply of methyl groups in the brain depends on a small number of nutrients working together: folate, B12, B6, choline, and methionine. If any one of those is deficient, the entire methylation economy slows. Homocysteine, an intermediate in the methylation cycle, accumulates. And homocysteine, as we will see in Chapter Six, is one of the most reliable predictors of brain atrophy that current science has identified.

Take a step back and look at the picture. The brain is a high-energy, high-turnover organ that is constantly remaking itself out of raw materials supplied by the diet. Those raw materials include long-chain omega-3 fats, B12, iron, zinc, iodine, choline, and a number of other compounds that the body cannot produce in adequate quantities on its own. The diet has to deliver them. When the diet is structured around plant foods alone, several of those nutrients become difficult to obtain. The biochemical machinery does not stop running, but it begins to run on increasingly thin reserves. Symptoms appear at first in the soft, hard-to-measure dimensions: mood shifts, brain fog, fatigue, slower processing speed. They appear later in harder dimensions: measurable cognitive decline, structural changes visible on MRI, elevated risk of neurodegenerative disease.

There is a particular feature of the brain that makes nutrient deficiency especially dangerous: the brain does not regenerate the way other tissues do. The skin sheds and replaces itself every few weeks. The lining of the gut renews itself every few days. The blood is constantly remade. The brain, by contrast, retains most of its neurons across a lifetime. There is some adult neurogenesis in specific regions, but the overall picture is one of long-lived cells that must maintain themselves for decades. When those cells lose their capacity to maintain themselves — because the raw materials are not arriving — the damage accumulates. A neuron that fails today is not replaced tomorrow.

This is why the brain mass story matters. When researchers find that low B12 status correlates with accelerated brain atrophy, they are not finding a temporary, reversible change in some easily replaced tissue. They are finding the loss of cells that will not be replaced. The brain that shrinks does not, in most cases, recover its lost volume. The cognitive function that erodes does not return. Whatever the long-term function of those lost cells was — memory, planning, motor control, language — it is degraded permanently.

The defenders of plant-only diets often point out that nutrient deficiencies are not unique to vegans. This is true. There are deficient omnivores. There are people who eat meat every day and still have inadequate B12 status because their stomach acid is low or their intrinsic factor production is impaired or they take a medication that interferes with absorption. The relevant question, however, is not whether nutrient deficiencies happen elsewhere. The relevant question is whether the diet under examination is statistically more likely to produce them. For the nutrients we will discuss in this book, the answer is overwhelmingly yes. A strict plant-only diet without supplementation is not merely one of many diets that can produce these deficiencies. It is the dietary pattern most reliably associated with them, by a wide margin, in every population that has been studied.

In the chapters that follow, we will look at each of the implicated nutrients in turn. We will see what they do in the brain, what happens when they fall below threshold, how reliably they fall below threshold in vegan populations, and what the evidence says about the long-term consequences. The case is not built on any single nutrient. It is built on the convergence of many.

Before we do that, however, there is one more piece of background to cover. Of all the nutrients implicated in the vegan brain story, one stands above the rest in both clinical importance and public health visibility. That is vitamin B12. The next chapter is devoted to it.

Chapter 3

The Nutrients That Built Our Brains

Before we descend into the clinical evidence, it is worth laying out, in plain language, the cast of characters. The argument of this book turns on a relatively small list of nutrients. They are the nutrients that the brain depends on, that animal foods deliver reliably, and that strict plant-only diets fail to provide in usable quantities. Each will get its own chapter later in the book. This chapter is the orientation. It is the rough sketch of who is who, before we look at any of them in detail.

The headline nutrient is vitamin B12. Cobalamin is unique among vitamins in that it is not produced by plants, by animals, or by fungi. It is produced exclusively by bacteria and certain archaea. Animals acquire it by consuming bacteria, by harboring B12-producing bacteria in their gut, or by eating other animals that have done so. Humans have a system of receptors and transport proteins that exists, in evolutionary terms, for the sole purpose of pulling B12 out of food and getting it into the blood and the central nervous system. The system is intricate and easily disrupted. Stomach acid releases B12 from the protein it is bound to. A protein called intrinsic factor, secreted by the stomach lining, binds the freed B12. The complex is then absorbed in the small intestine through a specific receptor. Any link in this chain can fail; with age, with autoimmune disease, with certain medications, the chain becomes less reliable. But the most common single reason for B12 deficiency in healthy young adults is the simplest one: not eating any food that contains it.

Plant foods do not contain B12. There are widely circulated claims about exceptions — about nori seaweed, about tempeh, about spirulina, about mushrooms grown on B12-rich substrate — but the claims do not survive close inspection. Most of the B12-like compounds in plant foods are inactive analogs that the human body cannot use, and that may even bind to B12 receptors and block real B12 from being absorbed. A small number of fermented and contaminated plant foods contain trace amounts of real B12, but the amounts are not reliable enough to base a diet on. The strict vegan who does not supplement will, over time, become deficient. This is not a contested claim. It is the consensus position of essentially every nutrition body that has examined the question.

The second nutrient on the list is omega-3, specifically the long-chain forms EPA and DHA. The brain is built from DHA. Plants do not contain DHA in any meaningful quantity. They contain ALA, a shorter-chain omega-3 that the body can, in principle, convert to DHA. The conversion rate is poor in healthy young men, slightly better in healthy young women, and it is suppressed by the high omega-6 content of typical Western diets. A vegan who relies on flaxseeds and walnuts for omega-3 is delivering ALA to a body that will convert only a small fraction of it to DHA. The alternative is algal oil, which is the same form of DHA that fish accumulate by eating algae; it is a real and useful supplement, but it is also expensive, often inconsistently taken, and not part of the dietary pattern of most vegans.

The third nutrient is iron. Iron is not absent from plant foods — in fact, leafy greens, legumes, and certain seeds are reasonably iron-dense. The problem is bioavailability. Iron in plants is non-heme iron, which the gut absorbs at perhaps two to twenty percent depending on the food matrix, the presence of inhibitors, and the iron status of the person. Iron in animal tissue is heme iron, which the gut absorbs at around fifteen to thirty-five percent and which is much less affected by inhibitors. The same milligram of iron, on the supplement bottle, behaves very differently in the body depending on its source. Vegans, particularly menstruating women, are at substantially elevated risk of iron-deficiency anemia, and the risk is not uniformly addressed by the iron-rich plant foods they are told to rely on.

Zinc, the fourth nutrient, follows the same pattern. Zinc is present in legumes, nuts, seeds, and whole grains, but it is bound to phytic acid in those foods and absorbed at lower rates than the zinc in animal tissues. Zinc is required for hundreds of enzymes, including several involved in neurotransmitter synthesis and in the regulation of synaptic plasticity. Mild zinc deficiency is more common than the popular discussion acknowledges, particularly among vegans who do not soak, sprout, or ferment their grains and legumes to reduce phytate content.

Iodine is the fifth. Iodine is concentrated in seafood and in dairy, with minor contributions from iodized salt. The brain requires iodine to make thyroid hormones, which regulate the metabolic rate of every cell in the body and are essential for fetal and infant brain development. Vegans who do not consume seafood, dairy, or iodized salt are at elevated risk of mild iodine deficiency, and even mild deficiency in pregnant women has been associated with cognitive deficits in their children.

The sixth is choline. Choline is required to make acetylcholine, the neurotransmitter most heavily implicated in memory and attention, and it is required to make phosphatidylcholine, a structural lipid in cell membranes. The richest dietary sources are eggs and meat. Plant foods contain some choline but in lower concentrations and in matrices that do not always release it efficiently. Choline was only formally classified as an essential nutrient by the Institute of Medicine in 1998, which gives some sense of how recently the field has come to take it seriously. Most Americans, vegan or not, do not meet the recommended intake. Vegans, predictably, fall short more often.

The seventh, eighth, and ninth nutrients in the cast are creatine, taurine, and carnosine. None of them is technically a vitamin, and the body can synthesize all of them in small amounts. The synthesis, however, is energetically expensive, and it does not always meet the brain's full demand. Creatine is required for the rapid regeneration of ATP in tissues with high energy demand — including the brain. Taurine is involved in osmoregulation, neuronal excitability, and the development of the central nervous system. Carnosine is an antioxidant and pH buffer concentrated in muscle and brain. All three are present at meaningful levels in animal foods and at trivial levels, or none at all, in plant foods. The vegan body produces them, but it does so under load. Several recent studies have found that creatine supplementation produces measurable improvements in cognitive performance in vegetarians and vegans, in a way that it does not in omnivores — a finding consistent with the hypothesis that vegan brains are running on lower creatine reserves.

Selenium, vitamin D, and vitamin B6 round out the list. Each has a role in the brain's antioxidant defenses, its hormonal regulation, or its methylation chemistry. Each is, in various populations, a known weak point in plant-only diets.

The reader will notice that this is a long list. That is the point. The popular discussion of vegan nutrition tends to focus on B12 as a single, isolated, easily supplemented exception. The reality is that B12 is the most acute deficiency, but it is not the only one. A strict plant-only diet creates pressure across an entire interlocking system of nutrients, several of which the brain cannot do without. A supplement program that addresses B12 alone leaves the rest of the system exposed.

It also leaves exposed a deeper issue, which is that the body does not absorb supplements in the same way it absorbs nutrients in food. Cofactors matter. Food matrices matter. Bioavailability is not a number on a label. The omnivore who eats a steak is not eating a B12 pill plus an iron pill plus a zinc pill plus a choline pill. They are eating a co-evolved package in which each of those nutrients arrives in a form the body has been refining for millions of years to absorb together. The vegan who supplements is eating the components disassembled. Whether the disassembled version is functionally equivalent is one of the central empirical questions of this book, and the answer, as we will see, is more complicated than the supplement industry would like.

We turn now to the evidence. The single most important study in the literature on vegan brain health is the one I mentioned in the preface: the Oxford brain atrophy work led by Anna Vogiatzoglou and David Smith, published in 2008. It is the study that put hard MRI numbers on a question that had previously been answered with vague clinical impressions. We start there.

The brain, in other words, is shaped to the food that built it.

II

The Evidence of Harm

On atrophy, B12, homocysteine, children, and mood.

Chapter 4

The Oxford Brain Atrophy Study

The study at the heart of this book was published in the journal Neurology in September 2008. The lead author was Anna Vogiatzoglou; the senior author was David Smith, a professor of pharmacology at the University of Oxford who had spent years investigating the relationship between B vitamins and dementia. The study did not set out to indict any particular diet. It set out to ask a more focused question: in older adults, does the level of vitamin B12 in the blood predict the rate at which the brain loses volume?

The design was straightforward. The researchers recruited 107 community-dwelling adults aged 61 to 87. None of them had dementia at baseline. Each underwent an MRI of the brain. Each had blood drawn and a panel of B12-related markers measured: serum B12, methylmalonic acid (an indicator of cellular B12 function), homocysteine (an indicator of methylation status), and holotranscobalamin (the form of B12 that is biologically available to tissues). The participants were then followed for five years. At the end of the follow-up, the MRI was repeated. The change in brain volume was calculated.

The result was unambiguous. Participants in the lowest third of B12 status at baseline lost brain volume at six times the rate of those in the highest third, after adjusting for age, sex, education, and several other variables. The relationship was dose-dependent. Lower B12, more atrophy. The signal held across multiple measures of B12 status, including the more sensitive holotranscobalamin assay. The researchers were careful to note that the participants were not, by clinical standards, B12 deficient. Their B12 levels were within the normal range. The atrophy effect appeared at levels that would not have triggered any intervention in routine clinical practice.

The finding deserves to be sat with for a moment. The participants were not vegans. Most of them were ordinary British retirees, eating an ordinary mixed diet. They simply happened to be at the lower end of normal for B12. And those at the lower end of normal lost brain tissue at a rate dramatically higher than those at the upper end. The implication for populations with frankly low B12 — populations in which average serum B12 sits at or below the threshold that would have placed someone in the lowest tertile of the Oxford study — is grim. Vegans, by every survey that has examined the question, are a population in which average serum B12 sits in or near that low range.

The Oxford finding was not isolated. It built on a substantial body of earlier work linking elevated homocysteine to brain volume loss and cognitive decline. Smith's group had previously shown that homocysteine predicted hippocampal atrophy. Other research groups, working with different cohorts, had shown that homocysteine predicted dementia incidence over time spans of five, ten, and fifteen years. The 2008 study tied the homocysteine evidence directly to its underlying nutritional driver: B12 status, with a smaller contribution from folate and B6.

The follow-up trial, published two years later, sealed the inference. Smith and colleagues randomized 271 older adults with mild cognitive impairment and elevated homocysteine to receive either a high-dose B-vitamin supplement (containing folate, B6, and B12) or a placebo, for two years. The B-vitamin group lost brain volume at half the rate of the placebo group. In the participants with the highest homocysteine at baseline, the protective effect was even larger; their atrophy rate was reduced by more than fifty percent. The treatment was just three vitamins. The protected tissue was the substrate of memory, attention, and executive function.

It is not common in nutrition research to see effects this large from a single intervention. Most nutrition trials show small effects, or null effects, or effects that vanish on replication. The B-vitamin trial showed a robust, dose-dependent, biologically coherent effect on a hard imaging endpoint. It was not a soft outcome like a self-reported memory questionnaire. It was the literal volume of brain tissue, measured in cubic centimeters, on a machine.

What does the Oxford line of work mean for vegans?

It means several things, and they are worth disentangling.

First, it establishes that B12 status — even within the conventional normal range — is a meaningful predictor of brain volume loss in older adults. A vegan who is told that their B12 level is within the normal range and therefore not a concern is being given clinically incomplete information. The Oxford data show that brain atrophy accelerates as B12 falls within the normal range, well before any clinical deficiency is diagnosed. The standard reference range for serum B12 was set decades ago, in the context of detecting frank deficiency disease (megaloblastic anemia, severe neuropathy). It was not set with brain volume preservation in mind. Several researchers in the field have argued, on the basis of the Oxford data and related work, that the lower bound of the reference range is too low — that levels often considered acceptable are actually associated with measurable harm.

Second, the Oxford line of work establishes that homocysteine is not merely a passive marker of B-vitamin status. The intervention trial showed that lowering homocysteine, by supplying the vitamins required to metabolize it, slowed atrophy. The implication is that homocysteine is part of the causal chain. Elevated homocysteine is not just a flag for low B12; it is itself, or it produces, the toxic exposure that accelerates the loss of tissue. We will look at the mechanisms in Chapter Six. The point here is that the marker is the messenger. When a vegan has elevated homocysteine, the elevation is not a number on a lab report. It is a measurable biochemical state that is, on the available evidence, eroding their brain.

Third, and this is where the public health story gets uncomfortable, the Oxford findings have not produced a corresponding shift in public messaging around plant-only diets. The mainstream discussion of veganism continues to treat B12 as a minor exception that is fully solved by taking a daily supplement. The Oxford work suggests, on the contrary, that B12 sufficiency is a moving target with serious neurological stakes; that the standard reference range is too generous; that the population most at risk of falling within or below the dangerous zone is the population most reliably told that supplementation will solve everything; and that the brain volume cost of getting the math wrong is being paid silently, in tissue that does not grow back.

A defender of plant-only diets might respond that the Oxford participants were not vegans and that we should not extrapolate. This is a fair objection on its face. But the extrapolation is constrained by simple biology. The variable being measured was B12 status. The mechanism implicated was the methylation cycle. There is nothing in the biology that should make vegans differently affected by low B12 than non-vegans with low B12. If anything, vegans are more affected, because their B12 deficiency, when it occurs, tends to be more severe and more chronic than the deficiencies seen in mixed-diet populations.

The studies that have looked specifically at vegan populations support the extrapolation. A 2003 study from Germany found that nearly half of vegans tested had low B12 status by sensitive markers. A British study cited by the Oxford group found similar rates. A 2020 review of B12 status across vegan populations worldwide reported deficiency rates ranging from eleven percent in carefully supplemented populations to ninety percent in unsupplemented ones, with a typical figure of around forty percent. Forty percent of a population sitting in the zone that the Oxford data identify as accelerating brain atrophy.

This is not a hypothetical concern. It is a measurable, ongoing, large-scale exposure of a population to a known accelerant of neurodegeneration. The fact that it has not produced a louder public health response says more about the politics of food than it does about the science.

Chapter 5

B12 — The Vitamin That Builds Nerves

To understand why B12 deficiency is so dangerous to the brain, one has to understand what B12 actually does inside the cell. The vitamin is not, as the popular conception sometimes suggests, a generic energy booster or an ill-defined nervous system supporter. It has two specific, irreplaceable jobs, and it cannot be substituted for in either of them.

The first job is the synthesis of methionine from homocysteine. This reaction sits at the center of the methylation cycle. Homocysteine, a sulfur-containing amino acid produced as a byproduct of normal protein metabolism, must be cleared. The principal route of clearance involves taking a methyl group from a folate molecule and attaching it to homocysteine to make methionine. The enzyme that performs this reaction, methionine synthase, requires B12 as a cofactor. Without B12, the reaction does not run. Homocysteine accumulates. Methionine production falls. And the downstream consequences ripple through the brain.

Methionine matters because it is the precursor of S-adenosylmethionine, or SAMe, which is the universal methyl donor of the cell. SAMe is required for the synthesis of phosphatidylcholine, an essential membrane lipid. It is required for the methylation of DNA and histones, which regulate gene expression. It is required for the synthesis of myelin basic protein, the structural backbone of the myelin sheath. It is required for the production of several neurotransmitters and the molecules that regulate them. When SAMe falls — and it falls when B12 is deficient, because methionine synthase has stalled — the cellular economy of methylation is impoverished across the board.

The second job of B12 is in the mitochondrion, where it serves as a cofactor for the enzyme methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. This enzyme converts methylmalonyl-CoA into succinyl-CoA, which feeds into the citric acid cycle and ultimately into ATP production. Without B12, methylmalonyl-CoA accumulates. Its precursor, methylmalonic acid, rises in the blood and the urine. Methylmalonic acid is the marker that allowed the Oxford researchers to detect B12-related cellular dysfunction even in people whose serum B12 looked normal.

These two failures — stalled methionine synthase, stalled methylmalonyl-CoA mutase — produce a cascade of downstream effects, several of which converge on the brain.

Consider myelin. Myelin is the white insulating sheath that wraps around the axons of neurons. It is what allows electrical signals to travel down a nerve fiber at high speed. The composition of myelin is dominated by phospholipids, particularly phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin, both of which require methylation reactions for their synthesis. When B12 is deficient and the methylation cycle is impaired, myelin maintenance falters. The sheath becomes thinner, less complete, more vulnerable. Nerve conduction slows. The classic neurological signs of B12 deficiency — the numbness and tingling in the hands and feet, the loss of vibration sense, the unsteady gait — are signs of demyelinating damage to peripheral nerves and to the spinal cord. The same demyelinating process can occur in the brain itself. In severe and prolonged B12 deficiency, MRI studies have shown white matter changes consistent with demyelination, sometimes mistaken at first for multiple sclerosis.

Consider homocysteine. When B12 is low and homocysteine accumulates, the molecule is not merely a passive marker of dysfunction. Elevated homocysteine has direct toxic effects. It damages the endothelial cells that line blood vessels, including the small vessels of the brain, contributing to cerebral microvascular disease. It promotes oxidative stress. It interacts with NMDA receptors on neurons in ways that can drive excitotoxic damage. Population studies have linked elevated homocysteine to a risk of Alzheimer's disease and vascular dementia that, according to recent reviews, is fifty to seventy percent higher than in those with normal homocysteine. Recall that the Oxford intervention trial showed that lowering homocysteine with B-vitamin supplementation slowed brain atrophy by half. Homocysteine is not just a number. It is, on present evidence, part of the toxic exposure that ages the brain.

Consider neurotransmitter synthesis. The methylation cycle, when functional, supports the production of dopamine, noradrenaline, serotonin, and several of the molecules that regulate them. When B12 is deficient and methylation slows, neurotransmitter synthesis becomes less efficient. The clinical correlate is mood. B12 deficiency has been linked, in multiple studies, to depression, fatigue, irritability, and cognitive sluggishness. Some of the depression seen in vegan populations is, on present evidence, attributable to subclinical B12 deficiency. The supplementation studies are mixed, but several have found that correcting B12 status improves mood symptoms in populations where deficiency is common. Whether this would generalize to a randomly chosen depressed vegan is uncertain, but the biological plausibility is high.

Now turn to the practical question. How easy is it for a vegan to maintain B12 status?

The naive answer is that it is easy: take a B12 supplement, problem solved. The real answer is more complicated.

The form of B12 in most supplements is cyanocobalamin, a stable synthetic compound. It is converted in the body to methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin, the two active forms required by the enzymes described above. The conversion is not always efficient. Some people, due to genetic variation or other factors, convert poorly. Other forms — methylcobalamin, hydroxocobalamin, adenosylcobalamin — are available and may be preferable in certain individuals.

Absorption is the next obstacle. The intrinsic-factor pathway in the small intestine has a saturable capacity. When a single dose exceeds about two micrograms, absorption efficiency drops sharply. The body does, however, absorb a small percentage of larger oral doses by passive diffusion. This is why high-dose oral B12 supplements (1,000 micrograms or more) are sometimes used to overcome absorption issues. They work, but they require consistency. A dose missed for a week is a dose missed.

Storage is the third factor. The liver stores B12 in quantities that can last, in healthy adults, for two to five years after a complete cessation of intake. This is why new vegans often feel fine for the first year or two even without supplementation. The deficiency develops slowly, as the liver stores deplete. By the time symptoms emerge — by the time blood markers shift, by the time fatigue or numbness or memory symptoms appear — the deficiency may have been silently advancing for a long time, with cumulative effects on tissues that do not regenerate.

This is the silent dimension of the vegan B12 problem. A person who became vegan in their twenties may have plenty of B12 in their early thirties. By their late thirties, the stores are running down. By their forties, biochemical deficiency is detectable, but symptoms are vague. By their fifties, brain atrophy is accelerating, in a pattern indistinguishable from accelerated aging. By their sixties, the cumulative tissue loss may be substantial, and the cognitive consequences may be attributed to age rather than to a slow, decade-long nutritional shortfall.

The argument is not that every vegan develops dementia. Most do not. The argument is that the population, on average, is being exposed to a known accelerant of neurodegeneration, and that the exposure is poorly monitored, poorly addressed, and poorly understood by the people undergoing it.

The popular vegan literature acknowledges B12 as a footnote. "Just take a supplement." The clinical literature, looked at honestly, suggests something less casual. B12 status is not binary. It is a gradient. Within the gradient, even mild and clinically unrecognized shortfalls correlate with measurable tissue loss. The supplement is necessary, but it is not always sufficient. Compliance falls. Forms differ in efficacy. Absorption varies. And the underlying physiology is not designed for a complete absence of dietary B12 to begin with.

Chapter 6

Homocysteine — The Silent Neurotoxin

Few molecules in the human bloodstream have the strange biography of homocysteine. It is not a vitamin. It is not a hormone. It is not a neurotransmitter. It is an intermediate — a transient amino acid that the body produces every time it processes the essential amino acid methionine, and that the body is supposed to clear within seconds. When the clearance system works, homocysteine never accumulates. The body shunts it back into methionine via the B12-dependent reaction described in the last chapter, or it shunts it down a different pathway involving B6 to make cysteine and ultimately glutathione. Either way, it does not linger.

When the clearance system fails, homocysteine lingers. And when homocysteine lingers, the brain takes damage.

The link between elevated homocysteine and neurological disease was first noticed in the 1960s, in patients with a rare genetic disease called homocystinuria. These patients have a defective enzyme in the homocysteine clearance pathway. They cannot metabolize homocysteine normally. Their blood levels run extremely high — often ten to twenty times normal. They suffer, among other things, premature vascular disease, mental retardation, and in some cases early dementia. The clinical picture made it clear that homocysteine, in large amounts, is harmful to the human nervous system.

For decades the assumption was that homocysteine was only dangerous in its rare extreme form. That assumption began to unravel in the 1990s and 2000s, as a series of large epidemiological studies showed that even modest elevations of homocysteine — within the range routinely seen in middle-aged and older adults — predicted vascular disease and cognitive decline. The Framingham Heart Study, the Rotterdam Study, the Hordaland Homocysteine Study, and several others all converged on the same conclusion. Homocysteine, even at levels considered subclinical, was a measurable risk factor for adverse outcomes in the brain.

What does homocysteine actually do?

The molecule is reactive. At elevated concentrations, it generates oxidative damage in the lining of blood vessels, including the small vessels that supply the brain. It interferes with the normal repair processes of vascular endothelium. It promotes inflammation. It contributes to the development of cerebral small vessel disease, the patchy, slow-burn injury to the brain's microvasculature that shows up on MRI as white matter hyperintensities and that contributes to vascular cognitive impairment in older adults.

Homocysteine also interacts with the central nervous system more directly. Its metabolites can act as agonists at NMDA receptors, the glutamate receptors that, when overactivated, drive a process called excitotoxicity. Excitotoxicity is one of the well-established mechanisms of neuronal death in stroke, in seizure, and in chronic neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's. A persistently elevated homocysteine level provides a low-grade excitotoxic background against which all the other stresses of aging are imposed.

There is also evidence that homocysteine interferes with the normal processing of amyloid-beta, the peptide whose abnormal accumulation is one of the molecular hallmarks of Alzheimer's disease. Several lines of work have shown that homocysteine elevation alters amyloid metabolism in directions that favor pathology. The relationship is not simple, and not all studies agree, but the convergent picture is one in which homocysteine is a chemical environment that makes neurodegenerative pathology more likely to emerge.

Now consider the link to diet. Homocysteine clearance depends on B12, folate, and B6. In Western populations, folate intake is generally adequate, particularly in countries where flour is fortified. B6 intake is usually adequate. The variable that most reliably predicts homocysteine elevation in healthy young and middle-aged adults is B12. When B12 is low, methionine synthase stalls, the homocysteine-to-methionine reaction slows, and homocysteine accumulates. The relationship is so reliable that homocysteine is sometimes used as a more sensitive marker of B12 status than serum B12 itself.

Vegans, on average, have higher homocysteine than omnivores. The 2003 EPIC-Oxford analysis of 689 men found that vegans had significantly elevated homocysteine compared with meat-eaters. A 2013 meta-analysis confirmed the pattern across multiple cohorts. The elevation is not trivial. In some studies, it places vegans squarely in the range that, in the Oxford brain atrophy data, predicts accelerated tissue loss in older adults. The biological picture is consistent. Diet is shifting B12 status downward. B12 status is shifting homocysteine upward. Homocysteine is generating measurable risk for the brain.

The follow-up question is whether the risk can be neutralized by supplementation. If a vegan takes B12, folate, and B6 in adequate amounts, can homocysteine be brought back to safe levels? The honest answer is: usually, in principle, with caveats.

Several intervention trials have shown that combined B-vitamin supplementation reduces homocysteine. In the Oxford trial described in Chapter Four, the supplemented group showed both a homocysteine reduction and a corresponding reduction in brain atrophy. In other trials, homocysteine was reduced but cognitive endpoints did not always improve. The results are heterogeneous. They suggest that lowering homocysteine in late life, after years of damage have accumulated, may be partially protective but is not a full reversal. The damage already done is not undone by the vitamin tablet that comes too late.

This points to the central problem with the standard vegan response to B12 concerns. The response is: take a supplement, your homocysteine will be fine, the brain risk is solved. The first part of the claim is largely true. The second part is partially true. The third part is unsupported. Homocysteine is a marker; reducing it from a high level to a normal level reduces the rate of further damage. It does not retrieve the tissue lost during the years before the marker was measured and corrected. The vegan who became deficient in their thirties and supplemented from their forties is not the same, on the available evidence, as the person who maintained B12 sufficiency throughout. The cumulative exposure was different. The cumulative damage may be different.

There is a further wrinkle. Homocysteine elevation, even at the upper end of the normal range, is now widely treated as cause for concern. Major laboratories define the upper limit of normal at around 15 micromoles per liter. The Oxford brain atrophy data, however, showed accelerated atrophy at homocysteine levels well below that threshold. Several researchers have proposed that the upper limit of normal should be lowered to around 10 or 11 micromoles per liter for older adults — a level at which atrophy is not yet accelerating. By that more conservative criterion, a substantial fraction of the adult population, vegan and omnivore alike, is in the risk zone.

What is true for the general population is more true for vegans. They run, on average, higher homocysteine levels. They are more likely to fall above the proposed conservative threshold. They are more likely to fall above the conventional threshold. They are, in short, more chronically exposed to the chemistry that the Oxford line of work has linked to brain volume loss.

This is the deepest disagreement between the case made in this book and the case made by mainstream vegan advocacy. The vegan literature treats elevated homocysteine as a manageable nuisance, easily corrected by a supplement. The neurology literature, looked at honestly, treats elevated homocysteine as a genuine pathological exposure that, over decades, contributes to measurable brain damage. The two communities have not yet had their full conversation. When they do, the implications for dietary advice will not be small.

Vegan children, particularly those raised vegan from infancy, are not theoretical cases. They exist. Their developmental outcomes have been measured. The picture is not reassuring.

Chapter 7

Children Raised Without Animal Foods

The argument of this book has so far been about adult brains: about the slow accumulation of damage over decades, about the silent erosion of tissue that the person carrying the brain may not notice until it is too late. There is, however, a population in which the same biology produces consequences that are neither slow nor silent. That population is children, and especially children whose mothers were vegan during pregnancy and breastfeeding. The data here are sharper, more disturbing, and more difficult for the vegan advocacy community to absorb.

The basic biology is the same, but the stakes are different. The infant brain is in a phase of explosive growth. Between conception and roughly age two, the human brain triples in size, lays down most of its myelin, and establishes the basic architecture of the regions that will support cognition for the rest of life. The raw materials required for this construction are large, and they are required on a continuous basis. The infant cannot wait for nutrients; the construction is happening now. A nutrient that is unavailable during a critical developmental window may not be replaceable later. The brain that should have been built does not, in some respects, get built.

B12 is the most acute example. The placenta concentrates B12, transferring it from the mother's blood to the fetal blood at higher concentrations than the mother's own. After birth, the breast milk of a B12-replete mother contains adequate B12 for her infant. But if the mother is herself B12 deficient — if she has been vegan, unsupplemented or inadequately supplemented, for years — neither the placenta nor the breast milk can supply what is not in her blood to begin with. The infant is born with depleted stores and feeds from a depleted source. Within weeks to months, the deficiency becomes clinical.

The clinical picture of severe infantile B12 deficiency is well documented and grim. The infant becomes lethargic, irritable, reluctant to feed. Developmental milestones are missed or lost. Some infants regress, losing skills they had previously acquired. Muscle tone is poor. The fontanelles can become unusually prominent, reflecting brain atrophy. MRI imaging in such cases has shown reduced brain volume, delayed myelination, and structural abnormalities consistent with disrupted neurodevelopment. In severe untreated cases, the damage is permanent. Even with prompt B12 replacement, some children do not fully recover. The brain that should have been built during those weeks of crisis was, in part, not built.

Cases of severe vegan-related infantile B12 deficiency have been reported in the medical literature for decades. Most often, the cases involve mothers who were unaware of the risk, or who believed that breast milk would supply everything the infant needed regardless of the mother's diet, or who had been told by their dietary advisors that supplementation was a precaution rather than a necessity. The cases continue to appear in the case reports of pediatric neurology journals. They are not historical artifacts. They are happening, in small but persistent numbers, in the developed world today.

The 2025 critical review of vegan and vegetarian diets in Nutrients summarized the evidence as follows: in infants and toddlers raised on vegan diets, B12 deficiency correlates with developmental delays, reduced brain volume, and impaired motor function, and maternal supplementation during pregnancy and lactation is essential. The wording is careful. The evidence underneath is not.

The hard-line vegan response to these cases is that the failures are individual rather than systemic — that any diet, executed carelessly, can harm a child, and that vegan diets, executed carefully, do not. There is some truth to this. The careful, supplemented, medically supervised vegan pregnancy is not the same thing as the unsupplemented one. But the careful version is the exception rather than the rule. Most vegans do not have ready access to obstetric and pediatric care providers who specialize in vegan nutrition. Most do not have detailed knowledge of the specific supplementation protocols that current research recommends. Many rely on advocacy materials produced by activist organizations rather than on clinical guidance, and many of those materials understate the risks.

The American Academy of Pediatrics and several European pediatric societies have issued statements over the years that, read carefully, are more cautious than the popular vegan literature. They acknowledge that a well-planned vegan diet can support healthy growth and development in children. The phrase "well-planned" is doing substantial work. It generally implies regular biochemical monitoring, professional dietetic support, and reliable supplementation — not just of B12 but of omega-3, iron, zinc, iodine, and vitamin D. It implies a level of vigilance that many families, vegan or otherwise, do not maintain.

Beyond B12, several other nutrients are particularly consequential in early development. DHA is required in large amounts during the late prenatal period and the first two years of life, when the brain is laying down its lipid architecture. Vegan mothers have lower DHA levels in breast milk than omnivorous mothers; their infants accumulate less DHA in their own tissues; and several studies have associated lower maternal DHA status with subtle developmental differences in offspring, ranging from changes in visual acuity to differences in cognitive testing scores at age four or five. The differences are not catastrophic, but they are measurable, and they are in the direction one would predict from the biology.

Iron is similarly critical. Iron-deficiency anemia in infancy and early childhood is associated with persistent cognitive deficits, even after iron status is corrected. The brain that develops under iron-deficient conditions is, on the available evidence, not the same brain that would have developed under iron-replete conditions. Vegan and vegetarian children, particularly toddlers, are at elevated risk of iron deficiency. The risk is increased further by the fact that plant iron is poorly absorbed and that the vitamin C-iron synergy that helps absorption is not always optimized in real-world feeding.

Iodine is a third. The thyroid hormones produced from iodine are essential for brain development; severe iodine deficiency in pregnancy is the world's leading preventable cause of intellectual disability. Even mild deficiency, in the range often seen in vegan mothers who do not consume iodized salt or seaweed, has been associated with measurable deficits in offspring cognition. The signal is small in any individual but real at the population level.

Choline is a fourth. The infant's developing brain is laying down phosphatidylcholine in vast quantities, and dietary choline supports the precursor pool. Vegan mothers, who eat no eggs and no organ meats, are typically below the recommended intake of choline. Whether this matters at the individual level is uncertain; the studies are sparse. But the biological plausibility is strong.

The cumulative picture is that a child raised in utero, in infancy, and in early childhood on a strict vegan diet — even with thoughtful supplementation — is being raised with a thinner margin of nutritional safety than a child raised on a mixed diet. The margin can be defended, if everything is done well. But the margin is real, and when things are not done well, the consequences appear in the brain of a child who had no part in the choice.

This is the population for whom the case made in this book is most acute. An adult who chooses a vegan diet for ethical or environmental reasons is making a choice about their own brain. They can read the literature, weigh the risks, accept whatever cumulative cognitive cost they are willing to bear. A child cannot. The infant whose brain growth is constrained by maternal nutrient deficiency was not consulted. The evidence that this happens, in real cases, in measurable numbers, is one of the strongest reasons to take seriously the central thesis of this book.

A defender of plant-only diets at this point will argue that we are talking about a small minority of badly informed cases and that they should not be used to indict the diet as a whole. I would accept the argument if the small minority were not measurable. They are. The pediatric literature contains a steady stream of case reports going back decades. The 2025 Nutrients review summarizes the pattern. The risk is real, the biology is understood, the corrective action is known. The question is whether the public conversation around veganism is presenting it accurately. I do not believe that it is.

Here the evidence is messier, the confounders are more numerous, but the pattern is, again, in the direction the biology predicts.

Chapter 8

Mood, Depression, and the Vegan Mind

The hard-edged data on B12, on brain atrophy, on infant developmental outcomes, are the strongest part of the case against strict veganism. They are also the rarest part. Most vegans do not develop megaloblastic anemia. Most do not develop frank dementia. The clinical extremes occur in a minority. What occurs in a much larger fraction is something subtler: a pattern of mood and cognitive symptoms that is real, measurable in population-level studies, and chronically underacknowledged in the popular discussion.

The cleanest signal is in depression. Multiple studies have found higher rates of depression and depressive symptoms in vegan and vegetarian populations than in matched omnivore populations. This is one of the more contested findings in the field, because the direction of causation is genuinely unclear. People with depression may be more likely to adopt a vegan diet for reasons unrelated to its effects on their mood — concern for animals, search for meaning, desire for control over one's life through control over diet. The diet may correlate with depression because it attracts depressed people, not because it causes depression. The research designs that would distinguish the two are hard to run.

Even so, several lines of evidence suggest that the diet is not merely a passive marker of depression risk but a contributor to it. Studies that have examined dietary patterns and depression in longitudinal designs have found that switching to a vegan diet sometimes precedes the emergence of depressive symptoms in people who were not previously depressed. Intervention studies in non-depressed populations have produced mixed but not encouraging results. A 2018 trial in healthy young adults found that switching to a vegan diet for several weeks produced no improvement in mood and, in some participants, a small worsening. A 2020 review concluded that the evidence on vegan diets and depression was insufficient to establish a causal relationship in either direction, but that the findings of higher depression rates in vegan populations were too consistent to dismiss.

The biological plausibility of a causal contribution is straightforward. Several of the nutrients implicated in vegan deficiency are also implicated in mood regulation. B12 deficiency, even subclinical, has been associated with depression in multiple studies. Folate deficiency has been similarly associated. EPA and DHA, the long-chain omega-3 fatty acids that vegans typically consume in low amounts, have been the subject of decades of research as adjunctive antidepressants, with consistent if modest evidence of mood benefits. Iron deficiency contributes to fatigue and low mood. Iodine deficiency, working through subclinical hypothyroidism, can produce depression-like symptoms.

The picture, in other words, is not that veganism is uniquely depressogenic in some mysterious way. It is that the specific nutrient deficiencies common in vegan populations are the specific nutrient deficiencies most strongly linked, in the biological literature, to mood symptoms. The diet does not need to be exotic in its mechanism. It works through known channels.

A 2021 paper in the journal Nutrients, examining a cohort of 9,668 adults from the UK Biobank, found that participants with the highest dietary vitamin B6, B12, and folate intake had measurably greater grey matter volumes in several brain regions associated with mood regulation, including the anterior cingulate cortex, the medial prefrontal cortex, and the superior parietal sulcus. Participants in the bottom range of intake — a range that, in the population studied, was disproportionately occupied by those with the most restricted diets — had reduced volume in those same regions. The finding ties together the structural and the symptomatic. The brain volume associated with mood regulation appears to track with intake of the specific vitamins that the strictest plant diets reliably underdeliver.

Beyond depression, the more subtle category is what is often called brain fog: a difficulty with concentration, mental fatigue, slower processing, and a sense that thinking itself has become less crisp. Brain fog is famously hard to measure. It is mostly self-reported. The literature on it in vegan populations is thin and mostly anecdotal. But the anecdotes are not absent. Online forums in which vegans discuss their experiences are full of accounts of brain fog that emerged after years on the diet, often in conjunction with fatigue, hair loss, or menstrual irregularities, and that resolved when the person reintroduced animal foods or began aggressive supplementation. These accounts are not science. But they are a signal that something is happening at the population level that the controlled studies have not yet fully captured.

Cognitive testing, the harder measure, has produced a mixed picture. Some studies find no significant difference in cognitive performance between vegan and omnivore populations. Others find small differences in specific domains — processing speed, working memory, recognition memory — that disfavor vegans. The differences, when they appear, are not large. They are within the noise of normal individual variation. They are the kind of differences that, in a single individual, would be invisible. They are the kind of differences that, applied across a population over decades, accumulate into something measurable on imaging.

Consider creatine, briefly. Creatine is concentrated in animal tissues. The body synthesizes some, but vegans run lower tissue levels than omnivores. A series of studies, beginning in the early 2000s and continuing through the present, have examined the effect of creatine supplementation on cognitive performance in vegetarians and vegans. The results have been remarkably consistent. Creatine supplementation produces measurable improvements in cognitive performance — typically in tasks of working memory and processing speed — in vegetarians and vegans, in a way that it does not in omnivores. The interpretation is straightforward. Vegan brains are running on lower creatine reserves than omnivore brains, and topping up those reserves recovers some lost capacity.

This is one of the more telling findings in the literature. It says, in effect: there is something the brain wants from animal foods that it cannot make in adequate amounts on its own; when you give it back, performance improves; the improvement is specific to people who were not getting it from food. This is not the pattern of a nutrient that is irrelevant. It is the pattern of a nutrient that is doing real work, and that the strict plant-only diet was failing to supply.

Similar patterns have been reported for taurine, carnosine, and choline, although the data are sparser. The cumulative implication is that vegan brains are operating, on average, with a thinner margin in several support systems. Each individual margin may be small. The cumulative effect, integrated over a lifetime, is what shows up in the brain volume data and the dementia incidence data and the homocysteine data and the mood data.

A defender of plant-only diets might argue at this point that the differences are too small to matter. Even if vegan brains are running a little less efficiently, even if depression rates are a little higher, even if cognitive testing scores are a little lower in some domains, none of these differences would change a person's life in any practical way. Most vegans go on to lead full, productive, happy lives. The claim is true and worth acknowledging.

But the claim ignores the populations for whom the differences matter most. A small reduction in cognitive reserve, in a young adult, is invisible. The same reduction, in an eighty-year-old who has been losing volume to age-related atrophy plus accelerated B12-related atrophy plus the cumulative effect of every other underdelivered nutrient, can be the difference between maintained independence and progression to dementia. A small mood disadvantage, in a happily settled adult, is a nuisance. The same disadvantage, in a young person navigating a major life transition, can be the difference between coping and a depressive episode. A small developmental difference, in a child who is otherwise loved and supported, is invisible. The same difference, in a child whose other risks accumulate, can shift the trajectory of their schooling.

Population-level signals matter at the individual level only sometimes. But over millions of people, over decades, even small signals translate into large absolute numbers of compromised brains. That is the population-level frame the chapter is asking the reader to take seriously.

Brain atrophy accelerates as B12 falls within the normal range, well before any clinical deficiency is diagnosed.

Chapter 9

Omega-3, the Brain's Building Block

If B12 is the vitamin that maintains the wiring of the nervous system, then docosahexaenoic acid — DHA — is the substance that builds the wiring in the first place. DHA is a long-chain omega-3 fatty acid with twenty-two carbons and six double bonds. It is exquisitely flexible at the molecular level, and that flexibility is precisely what neuronal membranes need. DHA-rich membranes change shape readily. They support the rapid rearrangement of receptors and channels that synaptic activity demands. The brain accumulates DHA in vast quantities, particularly in the synapses themselves. By dry weight, DHA accounts for something like ten to fifteen percent of the lipid content of the cerebral cortex. Remove DHA from the membrane and replace it with shorter, less flexible fatty acids, and the membrane works less well. The neuron works less well.

The biochemistry is unambiguous about where DHA comes from. The richest dietary sources are oily fish — salmon, mackerel, sardines, herring, anchovies — and other marine animals. Land animals contain smaller but meaningful quantities. Eggs from hens fed flaxseed or fishmeal contain enough DHA to matter. Dairy contains small amounts. Plants, with one exception, contain none.

The exception is algae. The DHA that fish accumulate is, ultimately, manufactured by marine algae and concentrated up the food chain. Algal DHA supplements, derived from cultured microalgae, are the principal way that vegans obtain pre-formed DHA. The supplements are real, they work, and they bypass the conversion problem we are about to discuss. The complication is that algal DHA supplements are expensive, are not part of the dietary pattern of most vegans, and are taken inconsistently when they are taken at all. The 2025 Nutrients review noted that even in well-supplemented vegan populations, blood levels of DHA tend to run substantially below those of omnivores, and that the gap is one of the most reliable nutritional signatures of the diet.

Plant foods do contain a precursor to DHA. The precursor is alpha-linolenic acid, ALA, an eighteen-carbon omega-3 found in flaxseeds, chia seeds, walnuts, hemp, and several other plant sources. The body, in principle, can elongate and desaturate ALA into EPA and then into DHA through a multi-step enzymatic pathway. This is the basis of the standard reassurance offered to vegans: you do not need fish; just eat your flax and your walnuts; your body will make what it needs.

The reassurance is not supported by the data. Conversion of ALA to DHA in healthy adults is, by every careful measurement that has been done, inefficient. The numbers vary across studies, but the typical conversion rate of ALA to DHA in adult men is around five percent or less. In women of reproductive age, who have a hormonal context that modestly favors the conversion, the rate runs a little higher, perhaps eight to nine percent. In men, in older women, in vegans whose diets are also high in omega-6 fatty acids that compete for the same enzymes, the rate is even lower. The conversion is rate-limited by the enzyme delta-6 desaturase, and that enzyme is saturated rapidly at modest substrate levels.

Consequently, increasing ALA intake beyond a certain point does not produce a corresponding increase in DHA. Eating more flax does not, beyond a small threshold, give you more DHA. The body simply does not have the enzymatic capacity to convert it. ALA, beyond that threshold, is metabolized for energy or stored in adipose tissue or used elsewhere; it does not arrive at the brain as DHA.

The result is that vegans run, on average, substantially lower blood and tissue DHA than omnivores. The deficit is documented in dozens of studies. It is one of the most reliable biomarkers of strict plant-only eating. The clinical question is what the deficit means.

Several lines of evidence suggest that it means something measurable.

Begin with development. The fetal and infant brain accumulates DHA at a rapid rate during the third trimester of pregnancy and the first two years of life. The DHA is supplied by the mother, transferred across the placenta and into breast milk. A vegan mother with low DHA status delivers less DHA to her fetus and her breastfed infant than an omnivore mother does. Several studies have measured DHA levels in the umbilical cord blood and in breast milk of vegan and omnivore mothers, and have consistently found lower levels in the vegan group. Whether this translates into measurable developmental differences in the infants is an active area of research. Some studies have found no detectable difference; others have found small differences in visual acuity or cognitive testing scores at age four or five. The signal is not enormous, but it is in the predicted direction.

Continue with adult cognition. Population studies have repeatedly found that higher dietary intake of DHA, or higher blood levels of DHA, predict slower cognitive decline in older adults. The Framingham study, the Rotterdam study, and several others have all shown the pattern. The relationship is not enormous, but it is consistent. Conversely, lower DHA status predicts faster cognitive decline. Vegans, who run lower DHA on average, are by extrapolation in the demographic that the longitudinal studies identify as at elevated risk.

Continue further with neurodegeneration. DHA appears to be protective against the development of Alzheimer's pathology in animal models, and several human studies have shown an inverse association between fish consumption and dementia incidence. The interventional evidence is weaker — randomized trials of DHA supplementation in cognitively normal older adults have produced inconsistent results — but the convergent epidemiological signal is real. The brain that is replete in DHA appears to be more resilient to the slow accumulation of neurodegenerative pathology than the brain that is not.

Add depression. EPA and DHA have been studied as adjunctive antidepressants for decades. The evidence is heterogeneous, but several meta-analyses have concluded that omega-3 supplementation, particularly with EPA-dominant formulations, produces a small to moderate benefit in major depressive disorder. The mechanism is not fully understood. It may involve membrane fluidity, neurotransmitter signaling, neuroinflammation, or some combination. The point is that the brain seems to operate, on average, slightly better when DHA and EPA are abundant than when they are scarce. Vegans, who are scarce by default, are forfeiting whatever modest benefit the abundant version confers.

Now consider the supplementation question. An attentive vegan could, in principle, close the DHA gap with daily algal oil. Several brands are available. The doses are well established. A daily intake of 250 to 500 milligrams of combined EPA and DHA from algal oil produces blood levels comparable to those of moderate fish consumers. The supplementation works.

The problem, again, is compliance and prevalence. Surveys of vegan populations consistently find that a minority — often well under half — take a regular DHA supplement. The majority rely on flax and walnuts and assume that their bodies will manage the conversion. Their bodies, as we have seen, mostly will not. The result is a population running chronically low on a structural component of brain tissue, with measurable consequences across the dimensions where the consequences have been looked for.

There is a final twist. The omega-3 to omega-6 ratio of the diet matters. Vegan diets, particularly those heavy in vegetable oils, can contain very high levels of omega-6 linoleic acid. Linoleic acid competes with ALA for the same enzymatic conversion machinery. A vegan who eats abundant flax along with abundant sunflower oil is delivering ALA into a system in which most of the enzymatic capacity is being used to process the omega-6. The conversion to DHA is suppressed further. The deficit deepens.

The implication is that even the small percentage of ALA that would otherwise be converted to DHA may, in real-world vegan diets, be reduced toward zero by the omega-6 background. This is one reason why the recommendation to "eat more flax" is, in practice, often ineffective. Without a corresponding reduction in omega-6 intake, the conversion does not get a chance to run.

The cumulative case for DHA insufficiency in vegan populations is, in my reading of the literature, as strong as the case for B12 insufficiency. It is just less acute. B12 deficiency produces dramatic syndromes — anemia, neuropathy, frank cognitive disturbance — that come to clinical attention. DHA insufficiency produces a slow, low-grade structural deficit that does not come to clinical attention but that, over decades, contributes to the gradient of brain health on which everything else operates.

We turn next to iron, and to the population that bears most of its burden.

Chapter 10

Iron, Cognition, and the Female Vegan

Iron is the third major node in the vegan brain story, and it is the one in which the demographic stakes are sharpest. The reason is biological. Menstruating women lose iron each month with menstrual blood. Their iron requirement is, accordingly, substantially higher than that of men. When the diet is restricted in a way that reduces iron bioavailability, women are the first to feel it.

Iron is not absent from plant foods. Lentils, beans, tofu, dark leafy greens, fortified grains, and certain seeds contain meaningful quantities of iron. The figure on the nutrition label, however, is a poor guide to what reaches the bloodstream. The iron in plants is non-heme iron, an inorganic form that the gut absorbs poorly and that is heavily affected by the surrounding food matrix. Phytic acid, which is abundant in legumes and whole grains, binds iron and reduces its absorption. Tannins, which are abundant in tea and coffee, do the same. Calcium, present in dairy and in calcium-fortified plant beverages, competes for absorption. Vitamin C, fortunately, increases non-heme iron absorption — which is why the standard advice is to combine iron-rich plant foods with citrus or peppers or another vitamin-C source.

The bioavailability of non-heme iron, in real diets, is typically estimated at five to twelve percent. The bioavailability of heme iron, the form found exclusively in animal tissue, is around fifteen to thirty-five percent. Heme iron is also less affected by the inhibitors that suppress non-heme absorption. The same milligram of iron, on the supplement label, behaves very differently depending on its source.

The consequence, well documented in dozens of studies, is that vegans and vegetarians have lower iron stores than omnivores. The 2025 Nutrients review reported that iron deficiency affects up to thirty percent of vegans in Western countries and up to sixty percent in regions like South Asia, where vegetarianism overlaps with already-marginal iron intake. The deficiency rates in vegan women specifically run higher than the overall figures, often by a substantial margin.

What does iron deficiency do to the brain?

The textbook answer focuses on hemoglobin and oxygen delivery. Iron is the central atom in the heme group that allows red blood cells to carry oxygen. Insufficient iron means insufficient hemoglobin means insufficient oxygen, and the brain, the most oxygen-hungry tissue in the body, suffers. The classic symptoms of iron-deficiency anemia — fatigue, exercise intolerance, headache, difficulty concentrating — are partly downstream of the oxygen problem.

The textbook answer, however, is incomplete. Iron has roles in the brain that are independent of hemoglobin. It is required for the synthesis of dopamine and other catecholamine neurotransmitters. It is required for the maintenance of myelin. It is required for the function of mitochondria in neurons, which is to say for the energy economy of the brain itself. Even before iron deficiency progresses to anemia — even when hemoglobin is still in the normal range — depleted iron stores affect the brain.

The cleanest demonstration of this comes from a series of studies in young women. Iron-deficient but non-anemic young women have been shown, in controlled studies, to perform worse on cognitive testing than their iron-replete peers. The differences are not enormous, but they are statistically reliable, and they appear in specific domains: attention, working memory, processing speed. A 2007 study cited in much of the subsequent literature found that iron supplementation in young women with depleted stores produced measurable improvements in cognitive performance, with the largest gains in those who started lowest. The improvements appeared at iron levels that would not have triggered any clinical intervention. The cognition was, so to speak, suboptimal at levels that the laboratory considered normal.

This is one of the recurring themes of the vegan brain story. The reference ranges of clinical chemistry were established to detect frank disease, not to define optimal function. A B12 level that is high enough to prevent megaloblastic anemia is not necessarily high enough to support optimal brain volume preservation. A ferritin level that is high enough to prevent anemia is not necessarily high enough to support optimal cognitive performance. The difference between the two thresholds — between not-clinically-deficient and adequate-for-the-brain — is where many vegan women live.

Pregnancy and infancy magnify the stakes. Iron requirements rise in the second and third trimesters of pregnancy. Iron deficiency in the mother increases the risk of preterm delivery, low birth weight, and impaired cognitive development in the child. Iron-deficient infants and toddlers, regardless of their mother's diet, show measurable cognitive deficits that may persist even after iron status is corrected. The brain that develops under iron-poor conditions is, in some respects, not the brain that would have developed under iron-replete conditions. Vegan mothers and vegan-fed children sit, on average, in a population in which the iron margin is thinner than it should be.

The standard vegan response to iron concerns is two-pronged. First, eat more iron-rich plant foods. Second, eat them with vitamin C to boost absorption. These are reasonable suggestions, and they help. But they are not enough for many people. A 2018 study of vegan women in the United Kingdom found that, despite consuming substantial quantities of iron-rich plant foods, a substantial fraction had low ferritin levels indicative of depleted stores. The food was reaching the plate. The iron, in many cases, was not reaching the cells.

Iron supplementation is the second line of defense, and it is widely used. The supplements work. But they have side effects that limit compliance. Constipation, stomach pain, nausea, and dark stools are common. Many women who start iron supplementation discontinue it within weeks. The result is a chronic, partially treated, partially untreated deficiency that, in some women, persists for years.

There is also the matter of inflammation. Iron metabolism is regulated by hepcidin, a hormone produced by the liver. Hepcidin rises during inflammation, and elevated hepcidin reduces the absorption of dietary iron and locks iron away in storage. Vegan diets are often promoted as anti-inflammatory, and on average they may be. But individual vegans with chronic low-grade inflammation — from gut dysbiosis, from autoimmune conditions, from the residual stress of life — may find that their inflammation is suppressing iron absorption regardless of intake. The story is not as simple as "eat more iron."

A particular subgroup of women deserves attention. Athletic vegans — runners, cyclists, dancers, anyone who combines intense exercise with restricted dietary intake — are at substantially elevated risk of iron deficiency. The combination of menstrual losses, exercise-induced losses (sweat, gut bleeding, foot strike hemolysis), and reduced bioavailability from a plant-only diet creates a perfect storm. The clinical literature contains numerous case reports of athletic vegan women presenting with profound iron-deficiency anemia and corresponding cognitive symptoms. These cases recover with treatment. The interesting question is how many subclinical versions of the same syndrome are walking around undetected, with mood and cognitive symptoms attributed to overtraining or stress rather than to nutritional shortfall.

Beyond the female-specific concerns, iron deficiency in vegan men is real but less common, because men do not have the menstrual losses that drive female deficiency rates. Older vegan adults of both sexes are at elevated risk, both because absorption efficiency declines with age and because the gut milieu becomes less hospitable to non-heme iron uptake. The pediatric vegan population is at risk, particularly during the transition from breast or bottle to solid foods, when iron requirements are especially high relative to caloric intake.

The iron story, then, runs parallel to the B12 and DHA stories. The biology is well understood. The deficiency rates are documented. The cognitive consequences are real, even at the subclinical end of the spectrum. The standard advice to eat more plant iron with vitamin C is helpful but, for many people, not sufficient. The supplementation that closes the gap has compliance problems and side effects. The result is a population, particularly a female population, running chronically thinner iron margins than is good for the brain.

The next chapter examines a set of nutrients that get even less attention than iron in the popular vegan literature: choline, creatine, taurine, and carnosine. Each of them is doing work in the brain. Each is found in animal foods at concentrations that plants cannot match. None of them is on the routine supplement list of most vegans. The cumulative effect of their absence is one of the under-examined dimensions of the vegan brain story.

Chapter 11

The Forgotten Nutrients — Choline, Creatine, Taurine, Carnosine

The vegan brain story does not end with the headline nutrients. There is a second tier of compounds — choline, creatine, taurine, carnosine — that are essential or near-essential for the brain, that are concentrated in animal foods, that are present at trivial levels in plant foods, and that are almost never on the supplement lists of mainstream vegan literature. Each of them is doing real work in the central nervous system. Their absence from the diet does not produce dramatic clinical syndromes, but it produces a chronic background of diminished capacity that, integrated over decades, contributes to the cumulative case being made in this book.

Begin with choline.

Choline was not officially classified as an essential nutrient by the United States Institute of Medicine until 1998. The reason for the delay was that the body can synthesize a small amount of choline from other building blocks, particularly through a methylation-dependent pathway. The synthesis, however, is slow and clearly inadequate to meet demand. The 1998 designation acknowledged what biochemists had long suspected: dietary choline is required for normal function, and the body's own synthesis cannot fully cover the gap.

Choline does several things. It is a precursor of acetylcholine, the neurotransmitter most heavily implicated in memory and attention. The drugs used to treat Alzheimer's disease — donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine — work by inhibiting the enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine, thereby raising acetylcholine levels in the brain. The clinical effort, in other words, has been to compensate for an acetylcholine deficit that plays a central role in dementia. A diet low in choline produces a smaller available pool of acetylcholine precursors. Whether this directly accelerates dementia risk is uncertain, but it is not biologically implausible.

Choline is also a structural building block of cell membranes, in the form of phosphatidylcholine, one of the most abundant phospholipids in animal cells. The brain, with its lipid-rich membranes, has a particularly high demand. During fetal and infant development, the demand is acute. Maternal choline status during pregnancy has been linked, in several studies, to cognitive outcomes in offspring. A landmark trial published in 2018 found that prenatal choline supplementation produced measurable improvements in offspring attention at age seven. The effect was not enormous, but it was statistically robust and biologically coherent.

The richest dietary sources of choline are eggs and beef liver, with meaningful contributions from other meats and dairy. Plant sources contain some choline — soybeans, broccoli, cauliflower, peanuts — but at concentrations that, in practice, leave most plant-only eaters short of the recommended intake of 425 milligrams per day for women and 550 milligrams for men. Surveys of choline intake in vegan populations consistently find that most participants fall well below these thresholds. The deficiency is silent. There is no clinical test that flags it routinely. There is no acute syndrome that brings it to a doctor's attention. But the choline that is not in the diet is, in real biochemical terms, choline that is not available to the brain.

Choline is also a methyl donor, like B12, and contributes to the methylation cycle that maintains DNA, neurotransmitters, and myelin. In a context where B12 is also deficient, the methylation deficit deepens further. Vegans, in other words, may be running on a methylation system that is short of two of its most important inputs simultaneously.

Move to creatine.

Creatine is concentrated in muscle tissue, where it serves as a rapid-access energy buffer for high-intensity work. It is also concentrated in the brain, where it serves the same function: a way to regenerate ATP rapidly during periods of high metabolic demand. The brain has substantial creatine demands. Sustained cognitive effort, like sustained physical effort, runs on ATP, and creatine helps to keep ATP available.

The body can synthesize creatine from amino acids — glycine, arginine, methionine — but the synthesis is energetically expensive and does not always meet full demand. Dietary creatine, from meat and fish, supplements the endogenous production. Vegans, who consume essentially no dietary creatine, run lower tissue creatine levels than omnivores. The deficit is well documented in muscle and, more recently, in the brain.

The most interesting evidence on creatine and cognition comes from intervention studies. A 2003 study by Caroline Rae and colleagues, published in the Proceedings of the Royal Society, tested creatine supplementation in vegetarians on a battery of cognitive tasks. The supplemented group performed measurably better than the placebo group on tasks of working memory and processing speed. The improvement was specific to vegetarians; later studies in omnivores found smaller or no effects. The interpretation is straightforward. Vegetarian and vegan brains are running on lower creatine reserves, and topping up those reserves recovers some lost cognitive capacity.

Subsequent work has reinforced the pattern. A 2018 systematic review concluded that creatine supplementation produces measurable cognitive benefits in vegetarian and vegan populations, particularly under conditions of cognitive demand or sleep deprivation. The benefits are modest, but they are real. The implication is again uncomfortable for the standard vegan position. If creatine supplementation produces cognitive improvements specifically in vegans, it follows that vegans, in the absence of supplementation, are running below their potential cognitive performance. This is not a hypothetical. It is a measured deficit that can be partially corrected.

Move to taurine.

Taurine is a sulfur-containing amino acid that is abundant in animal tissues and, like creatine, essentially absent from plants. It plays roles in osmoregulation, in bile acid conjugation, in mitochondrial function, and in the central nervous system. The brain contains substantial concentrations of taurine, particularly in developing tissue. Cats, famously, cannot synthesize taurine and develop blindness and cardiomyopathy on plant-based diets; they require dietary taurine for survival. Humans can synthesize taurine, and the synthesis is generally adequate in adults, although infants have limited capacity and rely more on dietary or breast-milk supply.

The concern in vegan populations is not that taurine deficiency produces a dramatic syndrome. It usually does not. The concern is that long-term vegan diets are associated with measurably lower tissue taurine levels and with a chronic dependence on the body's modest synthetic capacity. The downstream effects, if any, are not well characterized. There is some evidence that taurine has neuroprotective properties in animal models of neurodegeneration, but the human evidence is sparse. The honest position is that taurine is one of the under-studied components of the vegan brain story, with biological plausibility for an effect but limited direct human data.

Move to carnosine.

Carnosine is a dipeptide of histidine and beta-alanine, concentrated in muscle and brain. It functions as an antioxidant, a pH buffer, and a chelator of metal ions that would otherwise drive oxidative damage. Dietary carnosine comes almost exclusively from animal foods. Vegan diets contain effectively none. The body synthesizes carnosine from its constituent amino acids, but the rate-limiting step is the supply of beta-alanine, which is itself low in plant-only diets.

The interesting recent finding is that beta-alanine supplementation, which raises tissue carnosine levels, has been shown to improve cognitive performance under stress and fatigue conditions. The studies are mostly in athletic populations, but the mechanism is generalizable. Vegans, running lower tissue carnosine, are losing whatever modest neuroprotective and metabolic benefits the higher levels would provide. The deficit is not enormous in any individual, and the human cognitive evidence is preliminary, but the pattern is consistent with the broader picture: a series of small disadvantages in a population running thinner margins.

The cumulative point of this chapter is that the vegan brain story is broader than B12 and DHA. There is a second tier of nutrients — choline, creatine, taurine, carnosine — that the standard vegan supplement protocol does not address, that vegan diets reliably underdeliver, and that contribute, in measurable but individually small ways, to a diminished cognitive baseline. None of them, alone, would justify alarm. Together, layered onto the larger deficits in B12 and DHA and iron, they constitute the cumulative case for taking the diet's neurological cost more seriously than the popular discussion does.

The chapter that follows examines the trace minerals — iodine, zinc, selenium — that round out the cast.

Chapter 12

Iodine, Zinc, and Selenium — Trace Minerals, Major Stakes

Of the trace minerals implicated in vegan brain health, three stand out: iodine, zinc, and selenium. Each is required in small amounts, measured in micrograms or low milligrams per day. Each is reliably underdelivered by strict plant-only diets. Each has consequences for the brain that are larger than the modest dose requirement might suggest.

Iodine is the cleanest example, because its biology is the most direct. Iodine is required by the thyroid gland to produce thyroid hormones. Thyroid hormones — triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4) — regulate the metabolic rate of essentially every cell in the body. They are essential for normal brain development in the fetus and infant, and for normal cognitive function across the lifespan.

Severe iodine deficiency in pregnancy is, globally, the leading preventable cause of intellectual disability. The condition known historically as cretinism — characterized by severe cognitive impairment, growth failure, and deafness — is the result of maternal iodine deficiency severe enough to prevent normal fetal thyroid hormone production during the critical period of neurodevelopment. The introduction of iodized salt in many countries during the twentieth century dramatically reduced the prevalence of cretinism. The international public health community considers iodine deficiency one of the great preventable causes of cognitive harm in human history.

The reduction in severe deficiency, however, has not eliminated milder forms of the problem. Mild and moderate iodine deficiency, in which thyroid hormone production is reduced but not catastrophically so, produces less dramatic but still measurable cognitive effects in offspring. Several studies have shown that children born to mothers with mild iodine deficiency in pregnancy score, on average, slightly lower on cognitive testing than children born to iodine-replete mothers. The effect sizes are modest but real, and they accumulate at the population level into a meaningful number of children whose cognitive development was constrained by a deficiency that the system did not detect.

The richest natural dietary sources of iodine are seafood and dairy. Sea fish, shellfish, and seaweed concentrate iodine from seawater; dairy contains iodine because of the iodine-rich teat dips used in modern dairy farming and the iodine-fortified feed sometimes given to dairy cattle. Iodized salt, where it is used, provides a reliable supplementary source. The plant kingdom is, with the partial exception of certain seaweeds, generally low in iodine.

Vegans, who eat no seafood and no dairy, are at substantially elevated risk of iodine deficiency unless they actively use iodized salt or take an iodine supplement. Surveys of vegan populations in Europe and North America have consistently found that a substantial fraction — sometimes more than half — fall below the recommended intake. The deficiency is typically mild, not severe. The thyroid compensates by upregulating its uptake of available iodine and by producing thyroid hormones with the iodine it can obtain. Clinically apparent hypothyroidism is uncommon in vegans, but subclinical hypothyroidism — mildly elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone, normal-range thyroid hormone, fatigue and cognitive symptoms — is more common than the popular discussion acknowledges.

The complication is that some plant foods, paradoxically, contain compounds that interfere with thyroid hormone synthesis. Soy isoflavones, cruciferous vegetable glucosinolates, and certain other phytochemicals have goitrogenic effects in animal models, particularly under conditions of low iodine intake. Healthy adults with adequate iodine intake can consume these foods without consequence. Vegans with marginal iodine intake who consume large amounts of soy and crucifers may be compounding their thyroid stress. The interaction is one of the under-discussed elements of vegan nutritional planning.

Pregnancy, again, is the period of highest stakes. The recommended iodine intake rises from 150 micrograms per day in non-pregnant adults to 220 micrograms during pregnancy and 290 micrograms during lactation. Vegan mothers who do not consume iodized salt or supplements often fall well short of these elevated requirements, with consequences for fetal and infant brain development that are real but rarely traced back to their dietary cause.

Move to zinc.

Zinc is required by hundreds of enzymes in the body, including many involved in DNA synthesis, immune function, neurotransmitter metabolism, and the regulation of synaptic plasticity. The brain contains substantial concentrations of zinc, particularly in the hippocampus, where it modulates synaptic transmission and neural development. Zinc deficiency has been associated, in animal models, with impaired learning and memory; in human studies, with impaired growth in children and with subtle neurobehavioral effects in adults.

Plant foods contain zinc, particularly legumes, nuts, seeds, and whole grains. The bioavailability is reduced by phytate, the same compound that interferes with non-heme iron absorption. The bioavailability gap between zinc in animal foods and zinc in plant foods is not as extreme as the iron gap, but it is real. Vegan diets, on average, deliver slightly less absorbable zinc than omnivore diets, and a substantial fraction of vegan populations fall below the recommended intake when bioavailability is properly accounted for.

Soaking, sprouting, and fermenting plant foods reduces phytate content and improves zinc bioavailability. Traditional preparation methods in many cuisines incorporated these techniques. Modern vegan diets often do not. The practical consequence is that two vegans eating ostensibly the same foods can have substantially different zinc absorption depending on whether they prepare those foods in phytate-reducing ways. The advice to "eat a varied plant-based diet" obscures this distinction.

Zinc deficiency, like iron deficiency, has been linked to depression and to impaired cognitive performance, particularly in children. The signal is not enormous in adult populations, but it is real, and the population of vegans who run chronically low zinc is not small.

Move to selenium.

Selenium is incorporated into a small number of selenoproteins, several of which function as antioxidants. Glutathione peroxidase, the most studied of these, defends cells against oxidative damage. The brain, with its high oxidative load, depends on adequate selenium for its antioxidant defenses.

The dietary selenium content of plant foods depends entirely on the selenium content of the soil in which the plants were grown. In selenium-rich soils, plant foods can be excellent sources. In selenium-poor soils, they contain very little. Many parts of Europe, including substantial regions of the United Kingdom, Germany, and Scandinavia, have selenium-poor soils. Vegan populations in those regions are at elevated risk of selenium deficiency, particularly if they do not consume Brazil nuts, which are exceptionally rich in selenium and are often the staple plant source.

Selenium deficiency has been associated with cognitive decline in older adults, with mood disturbance, and with impaired thyroid function (since the conversion of T4 to T3 requires selenoenzymes). The effects are not as well characterized as those of B12 or iron, but the biological logic is consistent. A brain operating with depleted antioxidant defenses is a brain less able to resist the oxidative damage that accumulates with age.

The cumulative pattern of this chapter, like the pattern of the previous chapters, is that several individually modest deficiencies converge in vegan populations. None of them, in isolation, would justify a strong claim. Together, they form a coherent picture of a diet that systematically underdelivers a set of nutrients on which the brain depends for both structure and function.

Defenders of plant-only diets sometimes respond at this point by pointing to the rich antinutritional research on plant foods themselves: the polyphenols, the flavonoids, the carotenoids, all of which have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties that the standard omnivore diet underdelivers. The point is well taken and we will engage it in detail in Part Four. But the response is not symmetric. The plant phytochemicals, while real and beneficial, do not substitute for the missing animal-derived nutrients. They operate on different pathways, with different mechanisms, addressing different problems. A diet rich in polyphenols and deficient in B12 is not balanced; it is a diet with one strength and one critical weakness, and the brain has to manage both.

In the next chapter, we examine the antinutrients themselves: the phytates, the oxalates, the lectins, the goitrogens. These are not part of the absent-nutrient story; they are part of an active interference that plant-only diets impose on the absorption of what they do contain. The chapter completes the structural case for the diet's nutritional inadequacies. After that, we turn to the counterarguments.

Chapter 13

Antinutrients and the Active Interference

The chapters so far have concerned what is missing from a strict plant-only diet. There is a parallel story to tell about what is actively present in such a diet that interferes with the absorption of what little of certain nutrients does arrive. The compounds in question are the so-called antinutrients: phytates, oxalates, lectins, tannins, goitrogens, and several others. They are produced by plants, in many cases, as defensive chemicals against herbivory. They evolved precisely to make the plant tissue less digestible. The human gut handles them imperfectly.

The popular vegan literature often dismisses antinutrient concerns. Cooking, soaking, sprouting, and fermenting reduce antinutrient levels, the argument goes, and traditional cuisines have always handled these compounds. In a diverse, well-prepared plant-based diet, antinutrients are not a meaningful problem. There is some truth to this. There is also some falsehood. The antinutrient burden of a modern vegan diet is real, often higher than the optimistic accounts allow, and the cumulative effect on absorption of marginal nutrients is non-trivial.

Begin with phytic acid, sometimes called phytate. Phytate is the storage form of phosphorus in plant seeds, including legumes, grains, nuts, and seeds. It binds tightly to several mineral cations — particularly iron, zinc, and to a lesser extent calcium and magnesium — and forms insoluble complexes that the gut does not absorb. The mineral that is bound to phytate is, for nutritional purposes, lost to the body. It is excreted unchanged.

The phytate content of plant foods varies widely. Whole grains contain substantial phytate, concentrated in the bran. Legumes, before any preparation, are also high. Nuts and seeds contain meaningful amounts. Refined grains contain less because the bran has been removed, but refined grains have other nutritional disadvantages. The traditional preparation methods that have evolved across human cuisines — long soaking of legumes, fermentation of grains into sourdough, sprouting of seeds before consumption — all reduce phytate by activating endogenous phytase enzymes that break the molecule down. Modern cooking, with its short soak times, quick boiling, and reliance on canned or pre-cooked products, often delivers more phytate than traditional preparation.

The consequence is that the iron and zinc content of plant foods, as listed on a nutrition label, is often a poor predictor of the iron and zinc that the body actually receives. A bowl of unsoaked, unfermented whole-grain bread and lentils may deliver considerably less absorbable iron and zinc than the label would suggest. For a vegan whose iron and zinc intake is already marginal, the phytate burden can tip the balance from adequate to deficient. The 2025 Nutrients review specifically identified phytates and oxalates as antinutritional factors that may further impair nutrient absorption in vegan diets, necessitating careful dietary planning.

Move to oxalates. Oxalic acid is found in spinach, beet greens, rhubarb, swiss chard, and a number of other plant foods. It binds calcium, forming calcium oxalate, which is poorly absorbed and which, in concentrated form, contributes to kidney stones. For vegans who rely on greens as a calcium source, the oxalate problem is partially counterintuitive: spinach has impressive numbers on the nutrition label, but the oxalate content reduces the calcium absorption to a fraction of what the label suggests. Other low-oxalate greens — kale, collards, bok choy — are better calcium sources in practice.

Oxalates also contribute, in some sensitive individuals, to symptoms unrelated to calcium absorption. There is a small but real population of people with oxalate sensitivity who experience joint pain, urinary irritation, and other symptoms on high-oxalate diets. The vegan population overlaps with the demographic most likely to consume oxalate-heavy foods, and the cases occasionally come to clinical attention.

Move to lectins. Lectins are a heterogeneous family of proteins that bind to carbohydrate structures, including those on the surface of human cells. Some lectins are toxic; the lectin in raw kidney beans, phytohaemagglutinin, can produce severe gastrointestinal symptoms in even small quantities of undercooked beans. Most dietary lectins are inactivated by sufficient cooking, and the food supply is, on the whole, safe. There is, however, an active research literature on whether residual lectin exposure contributes to chronic gut inflammation and to broader inflammatory states. The popular literature on lectins, dominated by a small number of cardiologists and authors who have built careers on lectin avoidance, has overstated the case. The mainstream scientific assessment is that lectins, properly prepared, are not a significant health concern. But the topic is not fully settled, and a vegan diet, which is built on legumes and grains, delivers more dietary lectin exposure than an omnivore diet.

Move to goitrogens. Goitrogens are compounds that interfere with iodine uptake or thyroid hormone synthesis. They are concentrated in cruciferous vegetables (cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale) and in soy. In iodine-replete populations, the goitrogenic effect is negligible. In iodine-marginal populations, it can be additive. Vegans who consume large amounts of soy and crucifers — both of which are central to many plant-based diets — may be compounding the iodine pressure described in the previous chapter. The interaction is not catastrophic, but it is real.

Move to tannins. Tannins are polyphenolic compounds found in tea, coffee, red wine, certain fruits, and some legumes. They bind iron and reduce its absorption. The standard advice not to drink tea or coffee with iron-rich meals reflects this interaction. Many vegans do not follow the advice, particularly those who rely on tea or coffee for caffeine throughout the day. The effect on iron absorption is not enormous in any single meal, but, accumulated over thousands of meals over years, the contribution to chronic iron depletion is real.

There is one more category of antinutritional consideration that deserves mention: the saponins, the protease inhibitors, the alpha-amylase inhibitors, and the cyanogenic glycosides found in various plant foods. None of these is, in normal dietary quantities, a major health concern. Each of them, individually, makes a small contribution to reduced nutrient bioavailability or to subtle gastrointestinal stress. Cumulatively, in a diet that derives its entire caloric and protein content from plants, the antinutrient burden is meaningfully higher than in a mixed diet, and this contributes to the gap between intake on paper and absorption in fact.

A defender of plant-only diets at this point will reasonably argue that traditional preparation methods solve most of these problems and that the antinutrient story has been overhyped by anti-vegan polemicists. There is truth to this. Sourdough fermentation reduces phytate. Long soaking reduces phytate and lectins. Cooking inactivates most lectins and many enzyme inhibitors. A traditional, well-prepared plant-based diet — Indian dal made with soaked lentils, Korean fermented soy products, sourdough breads, sprouted grains — handles antinutrients far better than a careless modern vegan diet of canned beans and quick-cooked rice.

The reasonable concession is that the antinutrient problem is not a fixed feature of plant foods but a feature of how plant foods are prepared. The reasonable rejoinder is that most modern Western vegans do not prepare their plant foods in the traditional ways. They eat canned beans and pre-cooked grains and minimally processed plant proteins because their lives, like everyone else's, are time-pressed. The traditional preparation that the optimistic accounts assume is often not happening in practice.

Adding the antinutrient story to the missing-nutrient story produces the following picture. The strict plant-only diet underdelivers a set of nutrients that the brain depends on. Among the nutrients it does deliver, the bioavailability is often reduced by antinutritional compounds present in the same foods. The cumulative effect is a diet that, on paper, looks adequate and that, in practice, often is not. The gap between paper adequacy and real adequacy is one of the principal reasons that vegan populations show the deficiency rates described in the earlier chapters of this book.

We have now laid out the structural case. The brain is built and maintained on a list of nutrients. Strict plant-only diets reliably underdeliver several of those nutrients. The deficiencies are documented, the consequences are documented, and the cumulative weight of the evidence supports the central thesis: that the diet, as practiced by most of its adherents, imposes a measurable cognitive cost.

In the next part, we turn to the counterarguments. The defenders of plant-only diets are not without their own evidence. The case for the diet's protective effects against neurodegeneration is real, and it deserves to be engaged. The argument that supplementation closes the deficiency gap is widely repeated and deserves close examination. The argument that the bioavailability of plant nutrients has been understated also has its proponents. The case made in this book has to survive these counterarguments to be taken seriously, and that is what Part Four is for.

An adult who chooses a vegan diet is making a choice about their own brain. A child cannot.

The Counterarguments

On antioxidants, supplementation, and bioavailability.

Chapter 14

"But the Antioxidants" — Examining the Defense

A book that makes a critical case is obligated to engage the strongest version of the opposing case. In this chapter and the two that follow, I want to lay out the principal arguments that defenders of plant-only diets make in response to the kind of critique this book is offering. I will state each as fairly as I can, examine the evidence behind it, and explain why, in my reading of the literature, the counterargument does not fully neutralize the case made in the earlier chapters.

The first counterargument, and the most frequently invoked, is the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory case. Plant foods, the argument runs, are exceptionally rich in compounds — flavonoids, polyphenols, carotenoids, anthocyanins, and many others — that protect the brain against oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation. Oxidative stress and inflammation are central drivers of neurodegenerative disease. Whatever vegans may lack in B12 or DHA, they more than compensate with a flood of neuroprotective phytochemicals that the standard omnivore diet delivers in much lower quantities.

The argument is not nothing. The literature on plant phytochemicals and brain health is real, sizable, and in many places persuasive. The 2025 Nutrients review acknowledged that plant-based diets, when well planned, are rich in antioxidants and phytonutrients that have been associated with lower levels of inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein and interleukin-6, and that this anti-inflammatory profile may contribute to a reduced risk of neurodegenerative disease. Studies of the Mediterranean diet — which, while not vegan, is plant-leaning — have shown reductions in dementia incidence that are plausibly mediated by the diet's high content of polyphenols and unsaturated fats. Studies of specific plant compounds, like the flavonoids in berries, the curcumin in turmeric, or the catechins in green tea, have shown small to moderate cognitive benefits in various contexts.

The biological mechanisms are also reasonable. Polyphenols can cross the blood-brain barrier in modest quantities, where they act as direct antioxidants and as activators of cellular antioxidant defense systems. They can modulate the expression of inflammatory genes. They can support the gut microbiota in ways that affect the gut-brain axis. They can, in animal models, reduce amyloid pathology and improve cognitive performance. The case that plant phytochemicals are good for the brain is, in its general form, well supported.

So why does it not neutralize the case made in this book?

Several reasons.

First, the antioxidant and the missing-nutrient pathways do not address the same problems. Polyphenols help defend against oxidative damage. They do not provide the substrate for myelin synthesis. They do not provide the cofactor for methionine synthase. They do not provide the structural fatty acid for neuronal membranes. They do not provide the iron for hemoglobin or the iodine for thyroid hormone or the choline for acetylcholine. The argument that a vegan diet rich in polyphenols compensates for its B12 deficit is like the argument that a car with a high-quality paint job compensates for an empty fuel tank. The two systems do different things. Excellence on one does not substitute for failure on the other.

Second, the antioxidant case has been somewhat oversold. The most enthusiastic claims for individual antioxidants — that they will prevent cancer, slow aging, reverse cognitive decline — have repeatedly failed in randomized trials. Vitamin E and beta-carotene supplementation, which seemed promising on epidemiological grounds, produced disappointing or negative results in large interventional studies. The picture for plant-derived antioxidants in general is one in which observational associations have been more impressive than interventional outcomes. The mechanisms exist, the associations are real, but the magnitude of clinical benefit, when measured carefully, is often modest. The brain is not a simple chemical bath in which more antioxidants always equal less damage. The reality is more complicated, and the supposed neutralization of nutrient deficiency by phytochemical excess does not survive close inspection.

Third, the antioxidant content of a plant-based diet is not unique to vegan eating. An omnivore who eats abundant fruits, vegetables, herbs, spices, tea, dark chocolate, olive oil, nuts, and whole grains receives a polyphenol load comparable to most vegans. The Mediterranean diet, which has the most robust evidence for cognitive protection, includes fish, poultry, eggs, and modest amounts of meat alongside its plant-derived antioxidants. The cognitive benefits ascribed to the Mediterranean diet may be partly attributable to its antioxidant load, but they may equally be attributable to its DHA from fish, its B12 from animal foods, and its general nutritional completeness. The vegan diet does not have a monopoly on phytochemical richness, and an omnivore who eats well captures most of the same benefits without the deficiency risks.

Fourth, the supposed anti-inflammatory advantage of vegan diets is not as straightforward as it appears. Vegan diets typically lower CRP and other inflammatory markers compared with average Western omnivore diets, but the comparison is partly an artifact of the comparison group. Average Western omnivores eat large amounts of processed meat, refined carbohydrates, and industrial seed oils — a profile reliably associated with elevated inflammation. Compared with a thoughtful omnivore diet centered on whole foods, fish, and minimally processed meats, the inflammatory advantage of vegan diets is much smaller and not always present. The diet that the vegan literature compares itself to is often a strawman.

Fifth, several of the long-term cohort studies of plant-based eating and cognitive outcomes have been more equivocal than the popular discussion implies. Some have shown that strict vegetarians and vegans, in long follow-up, do not have lower dementia incidence than mixed-diet populations, and may in some studies have higher rates. The Adventist cohorts, often cited as evidence for the cognitive benefits of plant-based eating, are confounded by the broader Adventist lifestyle — non-smoking, non-drinking, religious community — which independently predicts better cognitive aging. When the lifestyle factors are partly controlled for, the dietary effect on dementia risk shrinks. The story of plant-based diets reducing neurodegenerative disease is less unified than the celebratory accounts suggest.

Sixth, even if the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant benefits of plant-based eating are real, they do not protect against the specific pathologies driven by nutrient deficiency. A vegan with elevated homocysteine is exposed to homocysteine-mediated vascular damage regardless of how many polyphenols they consume. A vegan with low DHA has thinner-membrane neurons regardless of their berry intake. A vegan with marginal iodine has an underactive thyroid regardless of how much green tea they drink. The protective and the pathogenic pathways operate in parallel. Strength on one does not erase weakness on the other.

This last point is the most important. The defenders of plant-only diets, in their strongest moments, write as though the diet's benefits and its costs cancel out, leaving a net advantage. The biology does not work that way. The benefits accumulate in the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory ledger. The costs accumulate in the structural-nutrient ledger. They are different ledgers. They produce different pathologies. The vegan brain may, on average, have somewhat better antioxidant defenses than the omnivore brain. It may also, on average, have somewhat lower DHA, lower B12, more homocysteine, less choline, less creatine. The two profiles do not produce a neutral sum. They produce a brain with some strengths and some specific, identifiable weaknesses, and the weaknesses are concentrated in dimensions — structural integrity, white matter health, neurotransmitter precursor availability — that determine long-term cognitive trajectory more than the strengths do.

There is a final consideration. The antioxidant case is largely about the prevention of disease in old age. The structural-nutrient case is about the building and maintenance of the brain throughout life. These operate on different time horizons. A small reduction in dementia risk in one's eighties, attributable to a lifetime of polyphenol consumption, is not nothing. But it does not compensate for the cumulative effect of decades of subclinical B12 inadequacy on cognitive performance in one's forties and fifties. The first benefit may exist; the second cost is also real. The accountancy is more complicated than the popular discussion allows.

The honest summary is this: the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory advantages of well-constructed plant-based diets are real, and they probably do contribute, modestly, to long-term brain health. They do not, however, neutralize the structural nutrient deficiencies that strict vegan diets impose. Both patterns are operating simultaneously in the same brain. The case made in this book is about the second pattern. The defenders of veganism are right that the first pattern exists. They are wrong to suggest that it makes the second pattern irrelevant.

In the next chapter, we examine the second great counterargument: that supplementation closes the gap. This is a more serious challenge to the case in this book, and it deserves a careful response.

Chapter 15

The Supplementation Problem

The most common response to the kind of critique made in this book is short and confident. Yes, the strict plant-only diet lacks B12. Yes, it lacks long-chain omega-3. Yes, its iron and zinc are less bioavailable than the animal-derived versions. So what? Take a B12 supplement. Take an algal oil capsule. Take an iron pill if your bloodwork suggests you need one. The problem is solved. Modern nutrition is built precisely on the principle that nutrients can be supplied independent of their food matrix. The vegan who supplements correctly has no nutritional disadvantage at all.

This is a serious argument. It deserves a serious answer. The answer, in my reading of the literature, is that supplementation is necessary for vegans, that it works partially, and that it is not the clean solution its proponents claim.

The first issue is compliance. Across all forms of medication and supplementation, in all populations that have been studied, long-term compliance is a persistent and substantial problem. Patients on prescription medications for conditions far more salient than nutrient sufficiency — cardiovascular disease, diabetes, asthma — show compliance rates of fifty percent or less over five-year time horizons. Compliance with vitamin and mineral supplementation, which is voluntary, less monitored, and less subjectively connected to immediate consequences, is typically lower than compliance with prescribed medication. Surveys of vegan populations have found that a substantial fraction — often more than half — do not consistently take a B12 supplement, and that an even larger fraction do not consistently take an algal oil supplement. The advice to supplement is a perfect solution in theory and a partially effective solution in practice.

A defender might respond that this is the vegan's individual responsibility and that compliance failures should not be used to indict the diet. There is some truth to this, but it overlooks an important point. If a dietary pattern requires lifelong, daily, conscientious supplementation to maintain neurological adequacy, and if that requirement is not consistently met by the people adopting the pattern, then the pattern, as practiced, has a higher real-world cost than its idealized version. Public health advice has to be evaluated against the population that will actually follow it, not against the population that would follow it under perfect conditions. The strict vegan diet, evaluated against actual compliance behavior, is one of the most demanding dietary patterns in terms of supplementation requirements, and the demand is often unmet.

The second issue is form. Different forms of B12, of iron, of omega-3 are absorbed and utilized differently. Cyanocobalamin, the cheapest and most common form of B12 in supplements, is converted in the body to the active forms methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin. The conversion is generally adequate but is impaired in some people, particularly those with genetic variants in the relevant enzymes. Methylcobalamin and hydroxocobalamin supplements are available and may be preferable in some individuals, but they are more expensive and less commonly used. The vegan who takes generic cyanocobalamin and assumes that all B12 supplements are equivalent is making an assumption that is not always correct.

Iron supplementation is similar. Ferrous sulfate, the cheapest form, has substantial gastrointestinal side effects that limit compliance. Iron bisglycinate is better tolerated but more expensive. Heme iron supplements derived from animal sources are best absorbed but are obviously not vegan. Plant-based iron supplements, at the dose often required to correct deficiency, frequently produce constipation, abdominal pain, and nausea severe enough to cause discontinuation. Many iron-deficient vegans cycle through several supplement attempts before finding one they can tolerate, and during the cycling their iron stores remain low.

DHA from algal oil is the cleanest of the vegan supplement options. The form is bioavailable. The dose can be matched to the equivalent of moderate fish consumption. The product is widely available. But it is expensive, often well above the cost of a fish oil capsule, and many vegans do not take it consistently. Algal oil supplements also tend to be lower in EPA than fish oil supplements; the body can convert DHA back into EPA at low rates, but the practical implication is that a vegan supplementing only algal DHA may be running lower on EPA than an omnivore taking fish oil.

The third issue is the interaction with food. Supplements deliver isolated nutrients. Foods deliver nutrients in matrices that affect their absorption and utilization. Some of these matrix effects favor the food version; some favor the supplement version. The honest answer is that the literature is mixed and that, for many nutrients, the bioavailability of food sources is somewhat higher than the bioavailability of equivalent supplement doses. The B12 in cyanocobalamin tablets is real and absorbable, but the B12 in animal foods comes packaged with cofactors and is delivered in a slow-release fashion through the digestive process. Whether the differences matter clinically is debated, but the literature does not consistently support the assumption that supplements are functionally identical to food sources.

The fourth issue is the reality of biomarker monitoring. The standard advice is that vegans should periodically check their B12, ferritin, omega-3, vitamin D, and other levels. In principle, this allows deficiencies to be caught early and corrected. In practice, biomarker monitoring is patchy. Most vegans do not have annual blood panels of the relevant markers. Many family physicians do not order the markers spontaneously, particularly the more sensitive ones like methylmalonic acid and homocysteine and holotranscobalamin. The serum B12 that is routinely measured is, as the Oxford research group showed, an insensitive marker of B12 sufficiency at the level relevant for brain volume preservation. A vegan can have a serum B12 in the lower-normal range, be assured by their physician that everything is fine, and be losing brain tissue at an accelerated rate. The monitoring system, as currently practiced, does not catch the slow-burn version of the problem.

The fifth issue is timing. The brain damage from chronic mild nutrient deficiency accumulates over years to decades. By the time a deficiency becomes clinically apparent, by the time symptoms emerge, by the time the supplementation is initiated or intensified, some of the damage has already been done. Tissue lost to demyelination does not always regenerate. Cumulative homocysteine exposure does not become uncumulative when homocysteine is later normalized. The supplementation that comes too late is not the same as supplementation that started early enough. Many vegans, particularly those who became vegan in their twenties and have followed the diet for decades without rigorous biomarker monitoring, are in a position where supplementation now is helpful but no longer sufficient to undo what has happened.

The sixth issue is the false sense of security that supplementation creates. A vegan who takes a daily multivitamin tends to assume, reasonably, that the multivitamin covers their nutritional bases. The assumption is often partially correct and partially wrong. Multivitamins typically contain B12, iron, zinc, and iodine, but at doses that may not be sufficient for vegan needs. They often do not contain DHA, choline, creatine, taurine, or carnosine. They do not address bioavailability issues. They do not always contain the optimal form of each nutrient. The vegan who relies on a generic multivitamin to neutralize the deficits of their diet may be undersupplemented across multiple dimensions without realizing it.

A final consideration is socioeconomic. Comprehensive vegan supplementation — high-quality B12, algal DHA, iron, vitamin D, iodine, zinc, choline, creatine — adds up to a meaningful monthly cost. For affluent vegans in well-resourced settings, this is manageable. For lower-income vegans, particularly in developing countries where vegetarianism is often an economic necessity rather than a lifestyle choice, the cost of full supplementation is prohibitive. The result is a population in which the adequacy of vegan eating is correlated with income. The middle-class American vegan with a Whole Foods budget can, in principle, eat well. The lower-income Indian vegetarian living on rice and lentils, who has been on the diet from birth, often cannot. The 2025 Nutrients review noted that iron deficiency in some plant-eating regions of South Asia approaches sixty percent of the adult population. The supplementation that solves the problem on paper does not always reach the populations who need it most.

The honest summary is that supplementation closes a substantial portion of the gap but does not close all of it. The vegan who supplements correctly is much better off than the vegan who does not. The vegan who supplements correctly is also, on the available evidence, somewhat worse off than the omnivore who eats a varied, nutrient-dense diet. The gap may be narrow, but it is not zero. And in any individual case, the assumption that supplementation has fully neutralized the deficit risk is difficult to verify without ongoing biomarker monitoring that most people, in practice, do not undergo.

The argument of this book is not that veganism is impossible to do safely. It is that doing it safely is harder than the popular discussion acknowledges, that most vegans do not in fact do it safely, and that the population-level consequences are larger than the optimistic accounts suggest. The supplementation argument, taken seriously, supports rather than refutes this conclusion. If supplementation were the simple solution it is sometimes described as, the population-level data on vegan B12 status, vegan iron status, vegan DHA levels, and vegan homocysteine would not look as bleak as they do. The data look bleak because the supplementation, in practice, is not solving the problem.

The final counterargument we will examine is the bioavailability one. The claim that plant nutrients are systematically less absorbable than animal nutrients has been challenged on its own terms, and the challenge deserves a careful response. That is the subject of the next chapter.

Chapter 16

Bioavailability — Why Plant Iron Isn't Animal Iron

A more sophisticated version of the pro-vegan argument concedes the deficiency points but pushes back on the bioavailability claim. The body, the argument runs, is highly adaptive. People who eat low-iron diets absorb a higher percentage of the iron they do consume. People who do not consume B12 develop more efficient enterohepatic recirculation. The bioavailability numbers cited in nutrition textbooks are based on populations not adapted to plant-based eating, and they overstate the gap that exists in well-adapted vegans. A long-term vegan, the argument concludes, is not a deficient omnivore minus animal foods. They are a different metabolic state, with different absorption patterns, in which the plant nutrient supply is more sufficient than the textbook numbers suggest.

This argument has some merit. There is real evidence that the gut adapts to dietary patterns over time. The expression of certain transporters increases under conditions of low intake. The fraction of dietary iron absorbed does rise as iron stores fall. The microbial community in the gut shifts to favor the breakdown of plant fibers. The body is not a passive recipient of nutrient delivery; it is an active, adaptive system.

But the argument, in its strong form, runs into a wall of empirical data that the adaptation does not break.

Begin with iron. The adaptive upregulation of iron absorption under conditions of deficiency is well documented. A person with low iron stores will absorb a higher percentage of dietary iron than a person with replete stores. The mechanism is the hormone hepcidin, which regulates iron absorption based on body iron status. Low iron stores suppress hepcidin, which increases the activity of the iron transporters in the gut. This is a real adaptation.

The adaptation, however, is partial. It increases absorption efficiency, but it does not eliminate the bioavailability gap. The 2025 Nutrients review and several earlier analyses have documented that, even in long-term vegans whose iron stores are low and whose hepcidin should be suppressed, iron deficiency rates remain substantially elevated compared with omnivores. The adaptive mechanism is doing its work, and it is still not sufficient to maintain iron sufficiency in a substantial fraction of the population.

The reason is straightforward. The bioavailability gap between heme and non-heme iron is too large for the adaptation to fully bridge. Heme iron is absorbed at perhaps three times the rate of non-heme iron, and the absorption is much less affected by inhibitors. A person whose absorption efficiency increases by fifty percent due to low iron stores still does not capture as much iron from a non-heme source as an iron-replete person captures from a heme source. The math does not work.

The same logic applies to B12. There is no meaningful adaptive mechanism that allows the human body to extract B12 from plant foods when the plant foods do not contain B12 to begin with. There is some recycling of B12 through the bile and the gut, with reabsorption in the small intestine, but the recycling is finite and does not, in the absence of dietary input, prevent eventual depletion. The hepatic stores that delay the onset of clinical deficiency in new vegans are eventually exhausted. The endogenous synthesis that some popular accounts suggest — based on B12-producing bacteria in the human gut — does occur to a small extent in the colon, but the B12 produced there is below the absorption sites in the small intestine and is largely excreted unabsorbed. Humans cannot meaningfully supply themselves with B12 from their own gut bacteria. The adaptive argument, applied to B12, is essentially false.

For DHA, the adaptive argument has been examined more carefully than for most other nutrients, and the results are not encouraging for the vegan position. Studies have measured ALA-to-DHA conversion in long-term vegans and compared it with conversion in newly converted vegans and in omnivores. The conversion rates do appear to be slightly higher in long-term vegans than in omnivores, suggesting some adaptation. The improvement, however, is small. Long-term vegans still convert ALA to DHA at low single-digit percentages. The improvement does not bring them to omnivore-equivalent DHA levels. The blood and tissue DHA in long-term vegans remains substantially lower than in fish-eating populations. The adaptation exists but is far from sufficient.

For zinc, the picture is similar. There is adaptive upregulation of zinc absorption under low-intake conditions, but the upregulation is bounded. Long-term vegans show somewhat better zinc utilization than would be predicted from naive bioavailability calculations, but they still show elevated rates of marginal zinc status compared with omnivores. The adaptation closes part of the gap and leaves part open.

The cumulative empirical pattern is that adaptation is real, modest, and insufficient to close the bioavailability gaps for the nutrients that matter to brain health. The vegan body does adapt. The adaptation does not adapt the brain out of needing the nutrients that the diet underdelivers.

There is a related argument that the bioavailability of plant nutrients has been measured incorrectly, that the laboratory methods used to estimate absorption do not accurately capture the real-world absorption that occurs over a long-term varied diet. The argument has some theoretical force — laboratory measurement of nutrient absorption is genuinely difficult — but it does not survive contact with the deficiency rates documented in real vegan populations. If the bioavailability of plant nutrients had been systematically underestimated, vegan populations would not show the deficiency rates that they do. The real-world data are the test of the theoretical argument, and the real-world data do not support it.

A subtler version of the bioavailability argument focuses on food preparation. Soaking, sprouting, fermenting, and cooking can reduce phytate content and improve mineral absorption. A vegan who prepares their food in traditional ways, the argument runs, has substantially better bioavailability than one who does not. This is correct, and it has been used appropriately in the previous chapters of this book to acknowledge that traditional plant-based diets handle antinutrients better than careless modern ones. But the argument concedes rather than refutes the broader point. It admits that bioavailability is a real problem and proposes a partial solution. The partial solution is helpful, particularly for diligent home cooks. It is less helpful for the modern vegan who eats canned beans, pre-cooked rice, and convenience plant proteins, which is the majority of the actual vegan population.

A different version of the argument focuses on metabolic differences between long-term vegans and omnivores. Long-term vegans, the argument runs, may have shifted their metabolic patterns in ways that change their nutrient requirements. Their cells may use less B12 per unit time. Their neurons may tolerate lower DHA membranes. The standard requirements were established in omnivore populations and may not apply to vegan ones.

This is an interesting argument, but it has a serious problem: there is essentially no evidence for it. The biochemistry of B12-dependent reactions does not change based on the eater's dietary preferences. The structural role of DHA in neuronal membranes does not change. The enzymatic machinery is shared across the species. There is no known mechanism by which a vegan's neurons require less DHA than an omnivore's. The hypothesis that long-term vegans somehow need less of the nutrients they consume less of is biologically unmotivated and empirically unsupported.

A final variant of the bioavailability argument focuses on the gut microbiome. The microbiome of long-term vegans differs from that of omnivores. The vegan microbiome produces more short-chain fatty acids, has different distributions of certain bacterial families, and has been associated in some studies with favorable metabolic profiles. There is genuine evidence that the vegan microbiome contributes to some of the diet's documented benefits, particularly in the cardiovascular and inflammatory dimensions. Whether it contributes enough to neutralize the structural-nutrient deficits is a separate question. The microbiome cannot manufacture DHA. It cannot produce bioactive B12 in absorbable locations. It cannot substitute for the heme iron that is not in the diet. The microbiome story is real and important, but it does not solve the brain-nutrient problem.

In summary, the bioavailability counterargument has merit at the margins. The body does adapt. Traditional preparation does help. Long-term vegans may extract somewhat more nutrients per gram of intake than naive calculations suggest. None of this comes close to closing the gaps documented in the deficiency literature. The vegan population, by every careful measurement that has been done, still runs lower in B12, lower in DHA, lower in iron stores, and lower in several other nutrients than the omnivore population. The adaptation is doing what it can. What it can do is not enough.

The cumulative weight of the three counterarguments — antioxidants, supplementation, bioavailability — is that they each contain a kernel of truth, that they each contribute partially to the case for the diet, and that none of them, alone or in combination, neutralizes the structural-nutrient case made in the earlier parts of this book. The defenders of veganism are right that the diet has real strengths. They are wrong to claim that the strengths erase the weaknesses. The two coexist in the same brain. The case made in this book is about the weaknesses.

Pregnancy, infancy, childhood, and old age are the periods of highest stakes, and they are the periods on which the closing chapters concentrate.

Chapter 17

Pregnancy, Infancy, Childhood

The argument of this book applies to all vegans, but it does not apply equally. The same nutritional deficits that produce subclinical effects in a healthy adult can produce serious consequences in a developing fetus, a breastfeeding infant, a growing child. The brain that is being built is more vulnerable than the brain that has already been built. The chapter that follows looks specifically at these vulnerable populations and at what the evidence says about them.

Begin with pregnancy. The fetal brain undergoes the most rapid period of growth in the human lifespan during the second and third trimesters. By birth, the brain has reached roughly twenty-five percent of its adult volume, a remarkable achievement in nine months. The construction is fueled by nutrients supplied by the mother. If the mother's nutritional reserves are inadequate, the construction is constrained.

For B12, the constraint is acute. The placenta concentrates B12, transferring it from maternal to fetal blood at higher concentrations than the mother carries. This is normally an evolutionary insurance policy: even a moderately deficient mother can supply her fetus adequately because of the placental concentrating mechanism. But the policy has limits. A mother who is severely B12 deficient — as many unsupplemented vegans are by their second pregnancy — does not have adequate B12 to be concentrated. The fetus receives what the mother has, and what the mother has is not enough.

The literature contains a steady stream of case reports of infants born to vegan mothers with severe B12 deficiency. The presentations are typically dramatic. Infants who appeared normal at birth begin to fail to thrive at two to four months. Developmental milestones are not reached. Some regress, losing skills they had previously acquired. Muscle tone deteriorates. Some develop frank neurological signs. MRI imaging in the worst cases has shown brain atrophy, delayed myelination, and structural abnormalities. Treatment with B12 produces partial recovery in most cases but full recovery in only some. The brain that should have been built was, in part, not built.

These cases are not common, but they are not negligible. The 2025 Nutrients review describes them as a recurring feature of the pediatric literature, particularly in cases where mothers were on strict vegan diets without supplementation, or where they assumed that breast milk would supply what their own diet did not. The cases are preventable. They occur because the prevention is not happening reliably.

For DHA, the consequences during pregnancy are real but more diffuse. DHA is concentrated in the fetal brain during the third trimester, and it is also concentrated in the developing retina, where it supports visual function. Studies of vegan and omnivore mothers have consistently found lower DHA levels in vegan cord blood and breast milk. Studies of the children, conducted at various ages, have produced mixed results. Some have found small differences in visual acuity at four to six months, with vegan-born infants performing slightly worse on standardized tests. Some have found small differences in cognitive testing scores at three to five years. Some have found no differences at all. The aggregate signal is small but in the direction the biology predicts. The honest summary is that maternal DHA insufficiency is unlikely to produce dramatic developmental deficits in any individual child, but it shifts the population distribution of developmental outcomes downward by a small but real amount.

For iron, the pregnancy literature is more clear-cut. Iron-deficiency anemia in pregnancy is associated with preterm birth, low birth weight, and impaired infant cognitive development. Iron-deficient infants and toddlers show measurable cognitive deficits that may persist even after iron status is corrected, in some cases into school age. Vegan mothers, who are at elevated risk of iron deficiency, are at elevated risk of these outcomes. The risk is partially mitigated by adequate prenatal supplementation, but the supplementation is not always adequate, and the iron deficiency rates in pregnant vegans, when they have been measured, are higher than in the general pregnant population.

For iodine, the pregnancy literature is alarming. Iodine deficiency in pregnancy, even mild forms, has been associated with measurable cognitive deficits in offspring. The thyroid hormones produced from iodine are essential for normal fetal brain development; even short windows of subclinical hypothyroidism in the mother can affect the developing nervous system. Vegan mothers who do not consume iodized salt or seaweed or take an iodine supplement are at elevated risk of subclinical iodine deficiency. The deficiency may not produce clinical symptoms in the mother. It can still affect the fetus.

For choline, the pregnancy literature is emerging. The 2018 trial showing that prenatal choline supplementation improved offspring attention at age seven was conducted in a general American population, not specifically in vegans. The implication, however, is that maternal choline supply matters. Vegan mothers, who consume no eggs and no organ meats and rely on plant sources of choline that are typically inadequate to meet recommended intake, are operating with thinner choline margins than their omnivore counterparts. Whether this produces measurable differences in their children is not yet fully known. The biology suggests it might.

The cumulative point is that vegan pregnancy is a period of magnified nutritional risk. The risks are mostly preventable through adequate supplementation and biomarker monitoring, but the prevention is often imperfect, and the cases where it fails produce consequences that are not always reversible.

Move to infancy. The infant brain triples in size between birth and age two. The growth requires a continuous supply of nutrients, supplied first by breast milk and later by the introduction of solid foods. A breastfeeding mother who is herself deficient cannot supply what she does not have. The 2025 review specifically noted that vegan-fed infants and toddlers show developmental delays, reduced brain volume, and impaired motor function in cases where maternal supplementation has been inadequate.

There is a second risk window when solid foods are introduced. Vegan-fed toddlers, transitioning from milk to a plant-only solid diet, are at elevated risk of iron deficiency, B12 deficiency if maternal status was already low, and low DHA. The complementary feeding of vegan toddlers requires substantial planning, and the planning is not always done well. Pediatric case reports of failure-to-thrive and neurological symptoms in vegan-fed toddlers are not rare in the literature, particularly when families have followed restrictive feeding patterns based on advocacy materials rather than clinical guidance.

Move to early childhood. Children aged two to ten are still growing rapidly, still constructing brain tissue, still vulnerable to the cumulative effects of marginal nutrient supply. The growth slows from infancy, but the developmental windows are still open. Iron-deficient school-age children show measurable deficits in attention, memory, and academic performance. Vegan children, who are at elevated risk of iron deficiency, are at elevated risk of these effects.

Adolescence brings another set of vulnerabilities. The pubertal growth spurt creates increased demand for protein, iron, zinc, and several other nutrients. The brain undergoes substantial structural reorganization during adolescence, with extensive remodeling of the prefrontal cortex and the limbic system. The nutritional inputs to this remodeling matter. Vegan adolescents, particularly those who came to the diet through ethical or social motivation rather than through family practice, often follow it inconsistently and with varying levels of supplementation. The results, when measured, show elevated rates of iron deficiency, lower B12 status, and lower DHA status compared with mixed-diet adolescents. The cognitive consequences in this population have not been well studied. The biology suggests that the consequences exist but are probably modest in any given individual.

The honest summary of the developmental literature is that vegan diets, executed with rigorous attention to nutrition and with full supplementation and biomarker monitoring, can support healthy development. Such execution is the exception rather than the rule. Most vegan pregnancies, vegan-fed infancies, and vegan childhoods do not receive the level of nutritional vigilance that the diet demands. The result is a population in which developmental outcomes are, on average, slightly worse than in matched omnivore populations, with a small subset of clearly compromised cases at the tail of the distribution.

The argument for paying attention to these populations is not the argument that veganism is uniformly harmful. It is the argument that the populations bearing the greatest risk are not the ones making the choice, and that the public health framing of vegan eating ought to take this asymmetry more seriously than it does. An adult who chooses the diet for themselves is making an informed decision about their own brain. A fetus, an infant, or a young child is being delivered into the consequences of that decision without consent. The duty of care is heavier in those cases, and the empirical evidence suggests that it is not always being met.

In the next chapter, we look at the other end of the lifespan: the aging vegan brain.

Chapter 18

The Aging Vegan Brain

The other end of the lifespan presents a different kind of vulnerability. The aging brain is not being built; it is being defended. Every year past the age of fifty, the human brain loses a small percentage of its volume to the cumulative effects of normal aging. The loss is gradual under good conditions and steep under bad ones. The conditions that determine the rate of loss are partly genetic, partly behavioral, and substantially nutritional. The vegan who reaches old age has spent decades exposing the brain to the dietary pattern this book has described. The accumulated cost begins to come due.

Consider the trajectory. A person who became vegan at twenty-five has, by the time they reach sixty-five, completed forty years on the diet. If their B12 status was marginal during those decades — and the population data suggest that for a substantial fraction of vegans it was — they have been operating with a chronically elevated homocysteine, a chronically suppressed methylation cycle, and chronically thinner reserves of the structural lipids the brain depends on. None of this produced obvious symptoms in any single year. Over forty years, the accumulated exposure looks different. The brain that started a normal volume at twenty-five may, by sixty-five, have lost more volume than the demographic average — not because the person is aging faster in any general sense, but because their brain has been running for decades on inputs that fell short of what the tissue required.

The Oxford brain atrophy data are the cleanest evidence for this trajectory. Recall that the original 2008 study found that older adults in the lowest tertile of B12 status were losing brain volume at six times the rate of those in the highest tertile. The participants were not vegans. They were ordinary British retirees who had drifted into low-normal B12 through a combination of declining absorption with age, occasional medication interactions, and unremarkable dietary patterns. Their B12 status was, on average, considerably better than the average vegan's. If this level of marginal B12 sufficiency in non-vegans produces six-fold accelerated atrophy, the implication for chronic vegans with frankly low B12 is, in any honest reading of the data, deeply concerning.

There is a second age-related vulnerability that compounds the vegan position. Stomach acid production declines with age. Atrophic gastritis, in which the stomach lining produces less acid and less intrinsic factor, becomes increasingly common after sixty. The condition impairs the absorption of dietary B12 even when intake is adequate. In an omnivore, the result is sometimes a slow drift toward subclinical B12 deficiency in the seventies and eighties. In a vegan whose dietary B12 intake is already minimal and who is dependent on supplement absorption, the same age-related decline in absorption capacity makes maintaining B12 sufficiency progressively harder. The supplement protocol that worked in the forties may not work in the seventies. Higher doses, sublingual or injectable forms, and more frequent monitoring become necessary. Most aging vegans do not adjust their supplementation in this way. They continue with the same daily tablet they have been taking for decades, while their absorption capacity quietly diminishes.

The dementia incidence data are where the cumulative concern becomes most visible. Multiple studies have found that elevated homocysteine in midlife predicts dementia incidence in late life, with risk ratios in the range of fifty to seventy percent above baseline for the highest homocysteine tertile. The implication for populations with chronically elevated homocysteine — which includes a substantial fraction of vegans — is that they are sitting in the demographic at elevated risk for the most catastrophic of all neurodegenerative outcomes. The risk is not certainty. Most vegans will not develop dementia. But the population-level shift is real, and it produces, in absolute numbers, a meaningful excess of dementia cases that would not have occurred under different dietary conditions.

The cognitive reserve concept is useful here. Cognitive reserve is the brain's capacity to absorb pathology without showing clinical symptoms. People with higher cognitive reserve — built through education, mental engagement, social activity, and underlying brain health — can sustain substantial neurodegenerative pathology before showing clinical signs of dementia. People with lower reserve cross the symptomatic threshold sooner. Brain volume is one of the contributors to reserve. A brain that has lost more volume to accelerated atrophy in midlife has less reserve to draw on as age-related pathology accumulates in late life. The same Alzheimer's burden produces clinical dementia earlier in a brain with thinner reserve. The vegan who has spent decades quietly losing accelerated volume to subclinical B12 deficiency may, if and when neurodegenerative pathology emerges, cross the threshold earlier than they otherwise would have. The dementia is not caused by the diet alone, but the diet has shortened the runway.

The cardiovascular dimension of dementia risk also matters. Vascular contributions to dementia are substantial. Cerebral small vessel disease — the patchy injury to the brain's microvasculature that produces white matter hyperintensities on MRI — contributes to cognitive decline directly and amplifies the impact of any concurrent neurodegenerative pathology. Elevated homocysteine is one of the contributors to small vessel disease. Vegans, with their elevated homocysteine, are at elevated risk for the vascular component. This is a different mechanism from the methylation-cycle-and-tissue-loss mechanism, but it operates in the same direction. Both pathways converge on cognitive decline in old age.

What about the protective factors? Vegans have, on average, lower rates of cardiovascular disease than omnivores in some studies, particularly when the comparison is with average Western omnivore diets. The cardiovascular benefits could, in principle, contribute to better cerebrovascular health and a reduced burden of small vessel disease. The signal here, however, is mixed. The EPIC-Oxford cohort and others have found that vegans have lower rates of ischemic heart disease but higher rates of hemorrhagic stroke, with the two effects partially offsetting in terms of overall vascular brain risk. Whether the cardiovascular advantage of vegan diets translates into a clear cerebrovascular advantage is not settled in the literature. The cumulative evidence does not support the strong claim that vegans, in old age, have demonstrably better brain vascular health than well-fed omnivores.

The dementia incidence studies in vegan populations specifically — as opposed to general plant-leaning populations — are sparse. The Adventist Health Studies, often cited in this context, do not find lower dementia rates in their vegetarian and vegan subgroups compared with their omnivorous Adventists. The difference between the two groups is small, and the broader Adventist lifestyle (no smoking, no alcohol, religious community, regular exercise) appears to be doing most of the protective work. Plant-based eating, isolated from the rest of the lifestyle, does not appear to confer a robust dementia advantage. This is one of the quieter findings in the field, and it is rarely featured in the popular discussion.

A particular subgroup of older vegans deserves attention: those who became vegan late in life, often for cardiovascular or animal welfare reasons. This group carries the cumulative omnivore brain reserve from their earlier decades, but they are now imposing on it the deficiency pattern of strict plant-only eating during the period when their absorption is already declining. The combination is unfavorable. Late-onset vegans should be subject to particularly careful monitoring of B12, iron, and homocysteine, with low thresholds for aggressive supplementation. In practice, they are often subject to less monitoring than younger converts, because their physicians do not always think to look for nutritional deficiencies in older patients with what looks like an otherwise healthy lifestyle.

The cumulative implication of this chapter is straightforward. The population in which the cognitive cost of strict veganism becomes most measurable is the older vegan population. The damage that accumulated quietly across midlife becomes visible in the brain volume changes, the cognitive performance changes, and ultimately the dementia incidence patterns of late life. The damage is real, the damage is preventable, and the damage is, in the populations that have been studied, only partially mitigated by the supplementation protocols currently in use.

This is the population for whom the case made in this book has the most direct practical implications. If you are sixty or older, have been vegan for many years, and have not had recent comprehensive biomarker monitoring including methylmalonic acid, homocysteine, holotranscobalamin, ferritin, omega-3 index, and vitamin D, the prudent step is to obtain that monitoring, interpret the results against the more conservative thresholds the contemporary research suggests, and adjust supplementation accordingly. The brain that you have at sixty-five is the brain you will be living with for the rest of your life. The cumulative cost of decades of marginal supply does not reverse spontaneously. The intervention that comes now may be partially protective. The intervention that does not come is, on the available evidence, allowing the underlying erosion to continue.

In the final chapter, we step back and summarize the case. The argument has accumulated across nineteen chapters of detail. It is worth reassembling at the end into the form of a single, clearly stated conclusion.

The brain that holds whoever you are deserves to be well fed.

Chapter 19

A Cautious Conclusion

The case has been laid out. It has not been laid out as a single dramatic claim but as the cumulative weight of many smaller ones. There is a moment, near the end of any serious investigation, when the analyst is obligated to step back from the detail and state plainly what the evidence supports. This chapter is that moment.

The evidence supports the following conclusions.

The human brain is a biochemically demanding organ that evolved over roughly two million years on a diet that included substantial animal foods. Several of the nutrients required to build, maintain, and operate the brain are either absent from plants entirely or present in forms the human body absorbs poorly. Vitamin B12 is the most acute example. Long-chain omega-3 fatty acids, particularly DHA, are a second. Heme iron, choline, creatine, taurine, carnosine, iodine, and several others fall on the same gradient.

A strict plant-only diet, executed without comprehensive supplementation and biomarker monitoring, reliably produces measurable deficiencies in several of these nutrients. The deficiency rates are documented across diverse vegan populations and across decades of research. The proposition that vegan diets produce nutrient gaps is not contested even by their defenders. The dispute is over how serious the gaps are and how easily they are closed.

The neurological consequences of these deficiencies are documented at multiple levels of evidence. At the level of biomarkers, vegan populations show consistently elevated homocysteine and consistently reduced indices of B12 sufficiency, DHA status, and iron stores. At the level of imaging, low B12 status predicts accelerated brain volume loss in older adults at rates approaching six times those of B12-replete peers. At the level of clinical outcomes, severe maternal and infantile B12 deficiency produces well-documented developmental harm. At the level of population epidemiology, elevated homocysteine in midlife predicts dementia incidence in late life with risk ratios in the fifty to seventy percent range above baseline.

The defenses raised against this picture are real but partial. The antioxidant and anti-inflammatory advantages of plant-based eating exist, but they operate on different pathways than the structural-nutrient deficits and do not neutralize them. The supplementation argument is correct in principle and incomplete in practice; compliance is imperfect, biomarker monitoring is patchy, the timing of correction often comes after damage has accumulated, and the supplements available are not always the optimal forms in the optimal doses. The bioavailability argument acknowledges some real adaptive capacity but does not bridge the gap, as evidenced by the persistent deficiency rates documented in long-term vegan populations.

The cumulative consequence of these findings is a measurable cognitive cost imposed by strict plant-only eating, distributed across the lifespan, with the heaviest burden falling on the most vulnerable populations: developing fetuses, breastfed infants, young children, pregnant women, and older adults whose absorption is declining. The cost is not catastrophic in any single individual. It is, at the population level, real, documented, and larger than the celebratory accounts of veganism are willing to acknowledge.

This is the case. It is the case I set out at the beginning of the book to make, and it is the case the evidence supports.

What follows from it?

Several things. None of them as dramatic as the strongest critics of veganism would prefer.

It does not follow that veganism cannot be done safely. The exceptional vegan, with detailed nutritional knowledge, comprehensive supplementation, regular biomarker monitoring, careful food preparation, and sustained discipline, can maintain neurological adequacy on a plant-only diet. The biochemistry permits it. The empirical question is what fraction of actual vegans operate at that level. The honest answer is that most do not. The dietary pattern, as it is actually practiced by most of its adherents, falls short of the requirements its advocates describe.

It does not follow that all vegans are damaging their brains. The damage is statistical. It exists at the population level. In any individual, the outcome depends on supplementation behavior, dietary detail, baseline nutritional status, age at adoption, duration of the diet, genetic factors, and a substantial residual of luck. A particular vegan may sit in the favorable end of the distribution and suffer no measurable cost. The fact that some do is consistent with the population-level finding that, on average, the diet imposes a cost.

It does not follow that the omnivore diet, as practiced by most modern Westerners, is the standard against which veganism should be measured. The standard ought to be a thoughtful, varied, mostly-plant diet that includes meaningful but moderate amounts of animal foods, particularly fish, eggs, and small amounts of meat or dairy. This is the dietary pattern that, on most current evidence, produces the best cognitive outcomes across the lifespan. It is not the strict vegan diet. It is also not the standard American diet. It is something in between, closer to the traditional Mediterranean pattern than to any pattern aggressively promoted in the contemporary food conversation.

What follows, more directly, is a set of practical implications.

For adults considering a vegan diet, the implication is to take the supplementation requirements seriously, in detail, with professional support if available. B12 supplementation is non-negotiable. Algal DHA supplementation is strongly recommended. Iodine, iron in many cases, vitamin D, and choline deserve attention. Periodic biomarker monitoring, including the more sensitive markers like methylmalonic acid and homocysteine and holotranscobalamin, is wise.

For pregnant and breastfeeding women, the implication is more cautious. The risk window is acute, the consequences of failure can be permanent, and the population in which the cost is borne — the fetus or infant — has not consented. The case for substantial caution about strict veganism during pregnancy and lactation is, in my reading of the evidence, strong. The case for at least temporarily relaxing the restriction during these periods, by including small amounts of fish, eggs, or dairy, is supported by the biology and the developmental literature.

For parents raising children on vegan diets, the implication is comparable. The evidence does not support the claim that healthy vegan childhood requires no special precautions. It requires substantial precautions, sustained for years, with professional support that most families do not have. The cases of harm in the pediatric literature are not historical curiosities. They continue to occur, in small but persistent numbers, when the precautions are not maintained.

For older adults who have been vegan for decades, the implication is to obtain thorough biomarker monitoring, interpret it against the more conservative thresholds the contemporary literature suggests, and adjust supplementation aggressively where needed. The damage accumulated across decades does not undo itself. The intervention that comes now is still partially protective.

For the general public conversation about diet, the implication is broader. The celebratory framing of veganism in much contemporary media is not, on the evidence, well calibrated to the diet's actual nutritional profile. The framing emphasizes ethical and environmental considerations, both of which have their own merit, while underplaying the neurological risks. A more honest framing would acknowledge that the diet has real costs as well as real benefits, that the costs fall most heavily on the most vulnerable populations, and that the benefits do not erase the costs.

I want to close with a personal observation about the spirit of the inquiry. The argument made in this book is critical, and the tone has been pointed at times. None of it is intended as contempt for vegans or for the values that lead people to adopt the diet. The ethical concerns about animal welfare are real, the environmental concerns about meat production are real, and the cultural pressures pushing toward less animal consumption have a substantial moral basis. Nothing in this book should be read as a defense of factory farming or of mindless meat consumption.

The argument is narrower. It is that the human brain is an animal organ, that the strictest version of plant-only eating fails to provide what it requires, and that the resulting cost — small in any individual, real at the population level, concentrated in the most vulnerable — is being paid more quietly than it ought to be. The honest discussion of veganism would acknowledge this cost openly. It would help people who choose the diet to do so with full awareness of what they are choosing and how to mitigate the risks. It would protect the children and the unborn who are not making the choice for themselves.

That is the discussion I have tried to contribute to. Whether the contribution succeeds is for the reader to decide. The evidence is on the table. The case has been made. The brain that holds whoever you are deserves to be well fed. What that means, at the end of two million years of evolution and at the beginning of a century of unprecedented dietary experimentation, is a question worth taking more seriously than the popular conversation has so far been willing to take it.

Reference

Glossary

ALA (alpha-linolenic acid)
The eighteen-carbon plant-derived omega-3 fatty acid found in flaxseed, chia, walnuts, and certain other plant foods. The body can, in principle, elongate ALA into the longer-chain DHA and EPA, but the conversion rate in adults is poor — typically below five percent in men and slightly higher in women. ALA is not a substitute for direct DHA in the diet.
Atrophic gastritis
An age-related condition in which the stomach lining produces less acid and less intrinsic factor, impairing absorption of dietary B12. Increasingly common after age sixty. Compounds the difficulty of maintaining B12 status in older vegans.
Bioavailability
The fraction of a nutrient consumed that is actually absorbed and utilized by the body. Varies widely by nutrient form, food matrix, and individual physiology. The bioavailability gap between heme and non-heme iron, between food-bound and synthetic B12, and between marine and plant omega-3 sources is a recurring theme of this book.
B12 (cobalamin)
A vitamin produced exclusively by bacteria. Essential for the synthesis of methionine from homocysteine, for the maintenance of myelin, and for mitochondrial function via methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. Absent in any meaningful form from plant foods. Universally recommended supplement for vegans.
Carnosine
A dipeptide of histidine and beta-alanine, concentrated in muscle and brain. Functions as antioxidant, pH buffer, and metal chelator. Dietary sources are exclusively animal. The body synthesizes carnosine, but the rate is limited by beta-alanine supply.
Choline
An essential nutrient required for synthesis of acetylcholine and phosphatidylcholine. Richest dietary sources are eggs and organ meats. Plant sources are generally inadequate to meet recommended intake. Officially classified as essential by the U.S. Institute of Medicine in 1998.
Creatine
A compound concentrated in muscle and brain that serves as a rapid-access energy buffer. Dietary creatine comes from meat and fish. Endogenous synthesis is partial. Supplementation has been shown to improve cognitive performance specifically in vegetarians and vegans, suggesting that the diet runs lower tissue creatine reserves.
DHA (docosahexaenoic acid)
The principal long-chain omega-3 fatty acid in the brain, accounting for ten to fifteen percent of cortical lipid by dry weight. Direct dietary sources are oily fish and, in supplement form, algal oil. Plants do not contain DHA in any meaningful quantity.
EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid)
A long-chain omega-3 fatty acid found alongside DHA in marine sources. Has well-documented anti-inflammatory and possibly antidepressant effects. Algal oil supplements typically contain less EPA than DHA, leaving supplemented vegans with proportionally lower EPA than fish-eating omnivores.
Folate
A B vitamin required, alongside B12, for the methylation cycle. Generally adequate in vegan diets due to high plant intake. Deficiency is uncommon in vegans except in cases of fortification absence or specific malabsorption.
Heme iron
The iron form found in animal tissues, where it is bound within porphyrin rings as part of hemoglobin and myoglobin. Absorbed at fifteen to thirty-five percent efficiency in the human gut, less affected by dietary inhibitors than non-heme iron.
Hepcidin
A hormone produced by the liver that regulates iron absorption. Suppressed when iron stores are low (allowing more absorption) and elevated during inflammation (suppressing absorption). Chronic low-grade inflammation can interfere with iron absorption regardless of dietary intake.
Homocysteine
A sulfur-containing amino acid produced as an intermediate in the methylation cycle. Cleared primarily through the B12-dependent reaction that produces methionine. Accumulates when B12 is deficient. Elevated levels are toxic to vascular endothelium and to neurons, and predict brain atrophy and dementia incidence.
Iodine
A trace element required for thyroid hormone synthesis. Concentrated in seafood, dairy, and iodized salt. Vegan diets that avoid all three are at substantially elevated risk of mild iodine deficiency, with consequences for thyroid function and, during pregnancy, for fetal brain development.
Iron
A trace element required for hemoglobin, neurotransmitter synthesis, myelin maintenance, and mitochondrial function. Plant sources contain non-heme iron, absorbed less efficiently than the heme iron in animal tissues. Vegan iron deficiency rates run substantially higher than omnivore rates, particularly in menstruating women.
Methylation cycle
A core biochemical pathway in which methyl groups are transferred between molecules. Required for DNA regulation, neurotransmitter synthesis, myelin maintenance, and many other functions. Depends on adequate supply of B12, folate, B6, choline, and methionine. Subject of much of the book's central concern.
Methylmalonic acid (MMA)
A metabolic byproduct that accumulates when B12 is functionally deficient at the cellular level. A more sensitive marker of B12 status than serum B12 itself. Elevated MMA, even with normal serum B12, indicates that the cells are not getting enough B12 to function optimally.
Non-heme iron
The iron form found in plant tissues. Absorbed at perhaps two to twenty percent efficiency depending on the food matrix and the body's iron status. Heavily affected by phytate, tannins, calcium, and other inhibitors.
Phytate (phytic acid)
The storage form of phosphorus in plant seeds. Binds iron, zinc, and to a lesser extent calcium and magnesium, reducing their absorption. Reduced by soaking, sprouting, and fermenting plant foods. Modern vegan diets often deliver more phytate than traditional plant-based cuisines.
Selenium
A trace element required for selenoprotein synthesis, including glutathione peroxidase, a major cellular antioxidant. Plant content depends on soil. Vegan populations in selenium-poor regions are at elevated risk of deficiency.
SAMe (S-adenosylmethionine)
The universal methyl donor in cellular biochemistry. Produced from methionine. Required for methylation reactions throughout the body, including those that maintain myelin and produce neurotransmitters.
Subclinical deficiency
A nutrient status that does not meet the criteria for clinical disease but that, on emerging evidence, is associated with measurable adverse outcomes. Much of the case in this book concerns subclinical rather than frank deficiencies.
Taurine
A sulfur-containing amino acid concentrated in animal tissues. Plays roles in osmoregulation, bile acid conjugation, neuronal excitability, and central nervous system development. Endogenous synthesis is generally adequate in adults but limited in infants.
Vitamin D
A fat-soluble vitamin produced in the skin under sunlight exposure and present in animal foods. Required for calcium metabolism and increasingly recognized for roles in immune function and brain health. Vegan diets often require supplementation, particularly in northern latitudes.
Zinc
A trace element required for hundreds of enzymatic reactions, including those involved in neurotransmitter synthesis and synaptic plasticity. Plant zinc is reduced in bioavailability by phytate. Vegan zinc deficiency is more common than the popular discussion acknowledges.

Notes on Sources

This book is a work of synthesis and critical interpretation. It does not contain a formal academic citation apparatus, but the major studies and lines of work referenced in the text are identified by author and year in the body of the chapters. This brief note is for readers who want to follow the trail further.

The single most important paper for the central thesis is Vogiatzoglou et al., "Vitamin B12 status and rate of brain volume loss in community-dwelling elderly," Neurology, 2008. The follow-up trial that demonstrated B-vitamin supplementation slowing brain atrophy is Smith et al., "Homocysteine-lowering by B vitamins slows the rate of accelerated brain atrophy in mild cognitive impairment: a randomized controlled trial," PLoS ONE, 2010. These two papers, taken together, are the empirical backbone of the brain-volume case made in Part II of this book.

The 2025 Nutrients review cited repeatedly throughout the text — examining nutrient adequacy, brain health, and developmental concerns in vegan and vegetarian populations — is among the most comprehensive recent critical assessments of the evidence base. Readers who want a single more academic overview of the same material should start there. The review consolidates findings on B12 deficiency rates, iron deficiency rates, DHA status, and the developmental literature in vegan-fed children.

For the omega-3 conversion literature, the foundational work was done by Burdge and colleagues at Southampton in the early 2000s, with subsequent confirmation in multiple cohorts. The review by Williams and Burdge in the British Journal of Nutrition is a useful entry point.

For creatine and cognitive function in vegetarians, Rae et al., "Oral creatine monohydrate supplementation improves brain performance: a double-blind, placebo-controlled, cross-over trial," Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 2003, is the original paper. Subsequent work by Benton and Donohoe extended the findings.

For prenatal choline supplementation and offspring cognition, the 2018 trial by Caudill and colleagues, published in the FASEB Journal, is the primary reference.

For homocysteine and dementia incidence, the relevant longitudinal studies include the Framingham Heart Study (Seshadri et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2002) and the Hordaland Homocysteine Study, which produced multiple papers across the 2000s and 2010s.

The case reports of severe B12 deficiency in vegan-fed infants are scattered across decades of pediatric neurology literature; representative examples include Roschitz et al. (2005) in the European Journal of Pediatric Neurology and Honzik et al. (2010) in the same journal.

For the EPIC-Oxford cohort, which has provided much of the population-level data on vegan health outcomes, the relevant papers are produced by the Oxford research group led by Tim Key and colleagues; their 2003 analysis on B12 and homocysteine remains a standard citation.

For brain volume and B-vitamin intake in the UK Biobank, the 2021 paper by Mosconi and colleagues in Nutrients is the primary reference.

Beyond these, the broader literature on each nutrient discussed — B12, DHA, iron, choline, creatine, taurine, carnosine, iodine, zinc, selenium — is extensive and easily searchable in databases like PubMed using the nutrient name and terms like "vegan," "vegetarian," "cognition," or "brain." Readers who want to verify or extend any of the claims made in this book are encouraged to do so. The case is not one that depends on cherry-picking; it is one that becomes stronger the more thoroughly the literature is consulted.

A final note on the limits of the evidence. Nutrition research is hard. Long-term randomized trials of dietary patterns are rarely possible. Most of the evidence on vegan brain outcomes is observational, which limits causal inference. The case made in this book has tried to acknowledge these limits openly while drawing reasonable conclusions from convergent lines of evidence. Readers who want certainty will not find it here, because the field does not yet provide it. What the field does provide is a coherent, biologically grounded picture, supported by multiple lines of evidence, of a dietary pattern that imposes measurable cognitive costs at the population level. That picture is the basis of the book's argument, and it is the basis on which thoughtful readers can form their own judgment.

There is no joy in writing a critical analysis of a way of eating that millions of thoughtful people have adopted for serious reasons. The ethical weight of the modern industrial meat system is real. The environmental cost of large-scale animal agriculture is real. The desire to live in a way that takes the suffering of other animals seriously is morally substantive, and it does not deserve dismissal from the pages of a book about brain chemistry.

What this book has tried to do is hold one specific corner of the larger conversation up to careful light. The corner concerns the human brain. The brain is not a moral agent in the conversation about animal welfare; it is a piece of biological tissue that has its own demands. Those demands evolved over millions of years on a particular nutritional substrate, and severing them from that substrate has consequences that the celebratory framing of veganism has not fully reckoned with. To say so is not to weigh in on the ethics of meat consumption. It is to insist that the empirical question be answered honestly, and the answer be allowed to inform whatever ethical conclusions are subsequently drawn.

There is, in my view, a version of the conversation that has not yet been fully had. It is the conversation in which the moral weight of vegan motivation is taken seriously, the environmental case for reducing meat consumption is taken seriously, and the neurological cost of strict elimination is also taken seriously. In that conversation, the conclusion may not be that strict veganism is ideal. It may be that a flexible plant-forward diet, one that includes occasional fish, eggs, or small amounts of meat — what some authors have called pescatarian, or flexitarian, or simply "mostly plant" — captures most of the ethical and environmental benefits without imposing the neurological costs. The dietary pattern that maximizes long-term cognitive welfare while honoring meaningful but not absolute restraint on animal consumption is a defensible target. It is the target that, on most current evidence, the science seems to point toward.

This is not the conclusion that the most committed advocates on either side of the food conversation want to hear. The strict vegan position rejects any animal consumption as a matter of principle. The committed carnivore position rejects any meaningful restriction. The flexible-mostly-plant position, which sits between them, is unsatisfying to both. It is also, on the evidence, more likely to be right.

I want to close with a thought about epistemic humility. The science of human nutrition is hard. Long-term randomized trials of dietary patterns are rarely possible. Most of what we know is observational, and observational evidence supports inference but does not prove causation. The case made in this book is supported by convergent lines of evidence that, taken together, are persuasive. It is not supported by the kind of incontrovertible randomized evidence that, in some other fields, settles a question definitively. A reader who finishes this book unconvinced is not unreasonable. A reader who finishes convinced is not unreasonable either. The honest assessment is that the case is strong but not airtight, and that further research may revise the picture in directions I do not currently anticipate.

What I do believe, with some confidence, is that the popular conversation about veganism has tilted toward celebration in ways that the underlying biology does not fully support, and that thoughtful skeptical engagement with the dietary pattern is overdue. This book has tried to provide some of that engagement. Whatever its limitations, I hope it contributes to a more honest discussion than the one that currently prevails.

The brain that holds whoever you are deserves a careful nutritional accounting. I have tried to provide it. The rest is yours to decide.
